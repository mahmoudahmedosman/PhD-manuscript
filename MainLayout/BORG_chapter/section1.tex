\section{Field level-inferences}
\subsection{Definition}
This definition is mostly taken from \cite{Leclercq_2025}. A field level inference generally refers to methods and techniques used to infer physical fields alongside a set of parameters from observations, without relying on compressed 
data from the corresponding field. As a result, a natural byproduct in cosmology is a cosmological map. These inferences are very high dimensional and require advanced statistical techniques. 
There are motives behind doing such analyses as they include more complexities than traditional summary statistics which could lead to less biasing results. 
For this PhD, we will be discussing field-level inferences specifically of matter densities and velocity fields. We will be doing so by resolving the inverse problem 
of how structures are formed from initial matter perturbations. The initial conditions (initial matter perturbations) will be denoted as $\delta^{IC}$. These 
initial conditions will then evolve to a final matter density field $\delta^F$ with an associated velocity field $\mathbf{v}$.  

Unlike the frequentist maximum likelihood approach adopted in the NaCl spectrophotometric framework, field-level inference requires a Bayesian 
statistical framework. In the frequentist approach, parameters are treated as fixed unknowns and inferred by maximizing the likelihood 
$\mathcal{L}(d|\theta)$, where $d$ is the observed data and $\theta$ are a set of parameters, 
with uncertainties estimated from Fisher information matrix. This is well suited for NaCl as the number of parameters can be handled by an personal computer. 
However, field-level inference involves a parameter space of order $N^3$ for an $N^3$ voxel 
grid. In our case $N$ can go from $64$ to $256$ giving us $10^5 - 10^7$ initial condition parameters alongside the model parameters 
rendering maximum likelihood estimation computationally intractable. 


The Bayesian framework addresses this by treating both data and parameters 
as random variables and combining the likelihood with a prior 
$p(\delta^{IC})$ using Bayes' theorem:
\begin{equation}
    p(\delta^{IC}, \theta, M | d) = p(M | d) \cdot p(\delta^{IC}, \theta | d, M)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    p(\delta^{IC}, \theta, M | d) = p(M | d) \cdot p(\theta | d, M) \cdot p(\delta^{IC} | d, M, \theta)
\end{equation}
where $M$ is a model of the Universe such as $\Lambda$CDM. The result is a full posterior distribution over the initial conditions. The posterior naturally distinguishes between regions of 
the density field that are well constrained by the data and those that are not, where the posterior defaults to the prior. The nuisance parameters 
are marginalised, integrated over their full posterior distribution, which correctly propagates all their uncertainty into the parameters of interest, unlike 
in the frequentist approach which explores only a restricted region of the full parameter space. 

An important feature of this approach is including a physically 
motivated prior exists for the initial conditions and the nuisance parameters. For the intial conditions, inflation predicts 
Gaussian primordial perturbations with a power spectrum $P_{\text{lin}}(k)$, giving us:
\begin{equation}
    p(\delta^{IC}) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} 
    \left (\delta^{IC} \right )^T \mathbf{C}^{-1} \left(\delta^{IC} \right) \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{C}$ is the covariance matrix. This makes the Bayesian framework not just computationally convenient 
but the physical natural choice for field-level inference.

\subsection{Motivations}

Unlike compressed statistics such as the matter power spectrum ($\left \langle \delta(\mathbf{k_1})  \delta(\mathbf{k_2})\right \rangle$), a field-level inference preserves all statistical information such as higher-order correlations. The structures 
observed today are non-linear, meaning correlations of higher order such as the bispectrum ($\left \langle \delta(\mathbf{k_1})  \delta(\mathbf{k_2}) \delta(\mathbf{k_3})\right \rangle$) 
or trispectrum ($\left \langle \delta(\mathbf{k_1})  \delta(\mathbf{k_2}) \delta(\mathbf{k_3}) \delta(\mathbf{k_4})\right \rangle$) are non-zero and must be calculated to extract maximal 
information on cosmological parameters. Since the field-level inference aims at reconstructing the entire underlying field as well as its structures, these correlations will always 
be accounted for at all orders. Moreover it preserves phase information. The compressed statistics mainly calculates the amplitudes of the correlations that exist while missing out 
on the phase for which these amplitudes are possible. We can assume that the contribution to the correlation function of two over dense regions would be the same had they been present 
anywhere in the Universe as long their separating distance remains the same. \\

Another motivation for field-level inference is reducing the uncertainties that arise due to cosmic variance at the local-Universe. Traditional summary statistics assume ergodicity 
i.e. averaged measurements over a sufficiently large volume of the Universe approximates the averages over many hypothetical realizations, which becomes problematic at 
the local Universe due to the limited volume. With field-level inference the goal is reconstruct the specific realization that produces our observations, allowing us to the 
properly adjust our model parameters to that realization. \\

The outcome of field-level inferences done on observations of our Universe is what we call a Digital Twin, a numerical simulation of the dark matter density field 
that should, in theory, match the density field of our observable Universe. 
Examples of such are the Manticore products obtained with the BORG forward model (\cite{Jens_2013}). Manticore-Local (\cite{Mcalpine_2025}) is the a set of reconstructed dark matter simulations to 
match the observations in the 2M++ galaxy catalogue with galaxies up to a redshift of $z \lesssim 0.1$. Manticore-Deep (\cite{Mcalpine_2026}) extends the work done on Manticore-Local 
by applying the algorithm on five galaxy catalogues 2M++, 6dFGS, 2dFGRS, SDSS, and BOSS reaching a redshift of $z \lesssim 0.7$, the largest Digital Twin that exists to date. 
This gives us access to more than just cosmological parameters but a better understanding of the local 
structures in the Universe by being able to observe them one by one like the Virgo cluster or the Great Attractor. 
It also allows us to access hard-to-observe properties such as the velocity field, formation histories and tidal fields. 
It can also be used to further the study of void catalogues 
which are sensitive probes to the dark matter fields as it was done in \cite{Malandrino_2026}. \\

\subsection{Examples of field-level inference frameworks}

There have been different methods developped in order to conduct a field-level inference over the past years. We will be mention a couple of those methods here. The first is the 
the FlowPM model (\cite{Modi_2020}), a differentiable cosmological N-body simulator using the Particle Mesh (PM) gravity solver (more details in Section \ref{sec:gravity_model}) 
designed to run on GPU for accelerated results using a neural network framework. It was shown to be able to reconstruct 
cosmological fields when implemented in a forward model Bayesian framework. Another model was developped by \cite{Simon_2025} based on the JaxPM\footnote{\url{https://github.com/DifferentiableUniverseInitiative/JaxPM}} 
gravity solver, an N-body simulator using PM coded in Python-Jax and can run on GPU. The model was tested using different samplers and they highlight the importance on carefully preconditioning 
latent variables in the model in order to achieve unbiased results. It also highlighted the differences between the samplers and the different algorithms that can be used to 
conduct an MCMC. More recently, works from \cite{Bayer_2026} demonstrated the capabilities of running a field-level inference when trying to reconstruct the BAO signal 
in comparison to traditional methods where a hybrid Effective Field Theory approach was applied instead of a PM gravity solver. The results have shown an improvement in the 
BAO signal reconstruction using field-level inference in comparison to standard methods. \\

\subsection{The BORG framework}

The \textit{Bayesian Origin Reconstruction from Galaxies} (BORG) (\cite{Jens_2013}) is a field-level forward model framework designed to reconstruct the initial conditions of the Universe given a set of observations. 
The structure formation model, or gravity model used, is a fast differentiable model named COLA (see Section \ref{sec:gravity_model}). It uses an explicit likelihood to relate 
the dark matter density field to observables. Examples of the applications of BORG are, as we have mentioned before the Manticore products (\cite{Mcalpine_2025,Mcalpine_2026}), 
but it has also been used in weak lensing catalogues (\cite{Porqueres_2023}) and the results from the Digital Twins have been heavily studied and compared to other probes to 
ensure consistency such as with with the Thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich Signal \cite{Stiskalek_2026} and with peculiar velocity catalogues (\cite{Stiskalek_2025_b}). 
Our aim during this PhD is being able to apply the BORG model on type Ia supernova. The results of our work are gonna be shown in Chapter 6. 
We can schematically display what a field-level inference with BORG on supernova would look like in Figure \ref{fig:BORG_schematic}. \\

\subsection{Field-level vs compressed summary statistics}

There have been multiple studies on the constraining power of field-level inference with respect to compressed summary statistics. We intend on showing some those results here. 
The first will be the work of \cite{Nguyen_2024} on the study of galaxy clustering at the field level. The paper focuses primarily on the constraints obtained on the $\sigma_8$ 
parameter. Two methods were used to constrain the parameter on two N-body dark matter mocks' halos. The first method uses a differentiable forward model that incorporates a non linear gravity 
solver LEFTfield (\cite{Schmidt_2021}) that combines Effective Field Theory and Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (see Section \ref{sec:gravity_model}) and evolves the inital conditions 
to match the simulated halos. The use of the fully evolved field gives direct access to the cosmological parameters. The second method extracts the cosmological parameters from 
the power spectrum and bispectrum of the mocks ($P+B$ method). The results obtained show improvements on the $\sigma_8$ constraints with the field-level inference with a factor of 1.9 to 5.2 
based on the limiting modes included in the analysis ($k_{\text{max}} = 0.10 h \text{Mpc}^{-1}$ and $k_{\text{max}} = 0.12 h \text{Mpc}^{-1}$ respectively)
and the mock used. Figure \ref{fig:nguyen} shows the constraints obtained from methods on one of the mocks. \\

Another analysis that highlighted the constraining power of field-level inference was done by \cite{Porqueres_2023}. The study was conducted on cosmic shear using the 
BORG framework. The developped hierarchical forward model developped included astrophysical systematics of intrinsic alignment and bayron feedback in addition to the gravity 
solver. The resulting model has been called BORG-WL. The model was tested on self-consistent simulations and was used to constrain both the matter content $\Omega_m$ and 
$\sigma_8$. This was then compared to the traditional summary statistics by infering the parameters using the angular power spectrum. They obtain the following results in 
Figure \ref{fig:porqueres}. The results show a much tighter constraints on both parameters as well as being able to break the degeneracy between them. \\
