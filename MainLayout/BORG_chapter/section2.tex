\section{The BORG Algorithm} \label{sec:BORG}

Within the BORG framework, we explore and sample the posterior for the large scale structure in the local environment, as captured by its initial conditions $\delta^{IC}$. We assume a 
Gaussian prior for these initial conditions.  
These 
initial conditions will then evolve to a final matter density field $\delta^F$ with an associated velocity field $\mathbf{v}$.
Modeling the evolving density field requires a gravity model that 
can capture 
the non linear effects of structure formation. We explain the details of the different parts of BORG in the next subsections. \\
\subsection{Distribution and Sampler}

\subsubsection{General Distribution}
The non-linear nature of gravitational structure formation makes it difficult to define an appropriate prior for the evolved density field (\cite{Fisher_1995}, \cite{Jasche_2010}). 
So even if one assumes a Gaussian or a log-normal for the $\delta^F$ they will be missing the description of fine filamentary features of the observed cosmic web (\cite{Peebles_1980}). 
Instead what is done in BORG is treat the problem as a Bayesian inference on the initial conditions. The biggest gain out of that is that the prior is physically 
motiviated from results obtained from Planck CMB data (\cite{Planck_2016}, \cite{Planck_2018}) and inflation which is a Gaussian prior. 
By combining this with a gravity model that evolves the density field and marginalizing over the resulting final density fields, we can constrain the underlying initial conditions. 
Equation \ref{eqn:bayesian_borg} shows the full posterior distribution for a field-level inference. For our analysis with BORG we will be restricting ourselves to $\Lambda$CDM, 
rendering $M$ constant. We will consider the joint posterior of the initial conditions and model 
parameters, $\mathcal{P}(\delta^{IC},\theta | d)$. Using Bayes theorem we get: 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(\delta^{IC},\theta | d) =
    \frac{
        \mathcal{P}(d | \delta^{IC},\theta)
        \cdot
        \mathcal{P}(\delta^{IC} | \theta)
        \cdot
        \mathcal{P}(\theta)
    }{
        \mathcal{P}(d)
    }.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{P}(\delta^{IC} | \theta)$ is the prior on the initial 
conditions, $\mathcal{P}(\theta)$ is the prior on the model parameters, 
$\mathcal{P}(d | \delta^{IC},\theta)$ is the likelihood and 
$\mathcal{P}(d)$ the model evidence. 

The CMB results mentioned previously motivate 
Gaussian primordial perturbations giving us: 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(\delta^{IC} | \theta) \propto
    \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}
    \left (\delta^{IC} \right )^T
    \mathbf{C}^{-1}
    \left(\delta^{IC} \right) \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{C}$ is the covariance matrix of the initial conditions. 

This posterior can be made explicit by specifying the form of the
data $d$ and the likelihood $\mathcal{P}(d|\delta^{IC},\theta)$. Following 
\cite{Jasche_2019}, we consider a dataset consisting of the number of 
observed tracers $N^{(i)}$ in each voxel $i$ of a grid. The gravity 
model $G$ maps the initial conditions to the final density field:
\begin{equation}
    G(\{\delta^{IC}_{(i)}\}) = \delta^F_{(i)}
\end{equation}
such that the likelihood $\mathcal{P}(d|\delta^{IC}, \theta)$ becomes the 
probability of observing $N^{(i)}$ tracers in each voxel given the 
evolved density field $\delta^F_{(i)}$. The full posterior then becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(\{\delta^{IC}_{(i)}\} | \{N^{(i)}\}) = 
    \frac{\mathcal{P}(\{\delta^{IC}_{(i)}\}) \cdot 
    \mathcal{P}(\{N^{(i)}\} | \{G(\{\delta^{IC}_{(i)}\})\})}
    {\mathcal{P}(\{N^{(i)}\})}
\end{equation}
where the Gaussian prior $\mathcal{P}(\delta^{IC})$ is the same as 
defined above, and the likelihood now explicitly depends on the initial 
conditions only through the gravity model $G$.

\subsubsection{Sampler} \label{sec:borg_sampler}

Given the dimensionality of the problem at hand, directly evaluating the 
high-dimensional integrals required to marginalize the posterior is 
practically infeasible due to the computational power required. 
The solution to that is to then explore the posterior distribution of initial conditions to find the best configuration set that matches our observations. 
This will require us 
to use a set of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling algorithms. 
A Markov Chain is a  sequence $\{ \theta_0, \theta_1, \theta_2, ..., \theta_n, ... \}$ where the conditional distribution of $\theta_{n+1}$ given the previous 
elements 
only depends on $\theta_n$. The goal of an MCMC algorithm is to obtain a numerical representation of the posterior distribution by drawing samples from it. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Metropolis-Hastings algorithm}} \\
The most common MCMC algorithm is the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm (\cite{Metropolis_1953}, \cite{Hastings_1970}). To summarize how it works: given a state $\theta_n$, a new state is drawn from a 
proposal distribution which follows a distribution $q$ such that: 
\begin{equation}
    \theta' \sim q(\theta', \theta_n) 
\end{equation}
If $q$ is a Gaussian distribution, we get $ \theta' = \theta_n + \epsilon; \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma)$ with $\epsilon$ being a random perturbation drawn from the normal 
distribution where $\sigma$ controls the step size. 
The next step is to then compute an acceptance ratio:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \text{min}\left(1, \frac{\mathcal{P}(\theta'|d)q(\theta_n | \theta')}{\mathcal{P}(\theta_n|d)q(\theta'| \theta_n)} \right)
\end{equation}

The state then becomes: 
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{n+1}=
    \begin{cases}
        \theta' & \text{With\; probability\;} \alpha \\
        \theta_n & \text{With\; probability\;} 1-\alpha \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

This guarantees that better proposals are more likely to be accepted while worse proposals have a probability proportional to how much worse they are. 
Moreover, under certain conditions this guarantees that the samples converge to the posterior. 
The problem BORG 
might have with this algorithm is the step size and the computation time since the complexity of the algorithm is $\mathcal{O}(D)$ where $D$ is the dimension of the parameter space. 
We expect BORG to have around $10^7$ parameters to fit so if the step size that is chosen is too small the chain will only make tiny steps and take a very long time to reach a converged state, while if the step size is too large most proposals will land 
in low-probability regions and get rejected, also making the computational time too long and inefficient. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{HMC algorithm}} \\
The Hamiltonian Monte-Carlo algorithm (HMC, \cite{Brooks_2011}) is much more suitable for our problem and is currently what is implemented in BORG. The general idea of HMC is 
to use concepts from Hamiltonian classical mechanics on high-dimensional problems. It uses gradient information to make significant proposals with a high acceptance rate. 
Let us define $\Theta$ as the position vector containing the set of parameters to solve, $\textbf{p}$ the momentum variables that have the same 
dimension as $\Theta$ and $\mathcal{H}$. The Hamiltonian, 
the Hamilton equations give us: 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}\Theta}{\text{d}t} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial \textbf{p}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}\textbf{p}}{\text{d}t} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial \Theta}
\end{equation}

with the Hamiltonian being constant in time. Let $\textbf{M}$ be the mass matrix which is symmetric and positive definite, then the Hamiltonian is: 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H} = \frac{1}{2} \textbf{p}^{\text{T}}\textbf{M}^{-1}\textbf{p} + U(\Theta)
\end{equation}
where $U(\Theta)$ is the potential energy and $\frac{1}{2} \textbf{p}^{\text{T}}\textbf{M}^{-1}\textbf{p}$ the kinetic energy. It is important to note that the choice of 
the mass matrix can strongly impact the performance of the sampler. 
At the beginning of each HMC iteration, the
momentum variables are randomly drawn from their conditional distribution
given the current position $\Theta$. For the quadratic kinetic energy defined
above, this distribution is a multivariate Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(\textbf{p}|\Theta)
    \propto
    \exp\left(
    -\frac{1}{2}
    \textbf{p}^{\text{T}}\textbf{M}^{-1}\textbf{p}
    \right),
\end{equation}
such that
\begin{equation}
    \textbf{p} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{M}).
\end{equation}
The momentum variables are auxiliary variables and are resampled at each
iteration to generate a new Hamiltonian trajectory; they are not parameters
of interest.

The link between Hamiltonian dynamics and probabilities can be established with the concept of canonical distributions from statistical mechanics. The probability 
distribution over the states and momenta are written as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) = \frac{1}{Z} \text{exp}\left(\frac{-\mathcal{H}}{k_B T} \right)
\end{equation}

where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ the temperature of the system and $Z$ is the partition function. We can impose $k_B T=1$ for simplicity and 
absorbing the $Z$ partition function into the normalization which leads us to:
\begin{equation}
    -\ln \mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) = \mathcal{H}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) = \frac{1}{2} \textbf{p}^{\text{T}}\textbf{M}^{-1}\textbf{p} + U(\Theta)
\end{equation}

We can see that the Hamiltonian formalism naturally allows the parameters $\Theta$ to be displaced along trajectories of a constant $\mathcal{H}$, guided by the gradient 
of the potential energy $ U (\Theta) = -\ln \mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p})$ towards regions of higher posterior probability. This means that the joint distribution 
is:  
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) \propto \exp(-\mathcal{H}(\Theta, \textbf{p}))
\end{equation}
We can then use the same acceptance condition as the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm where we have:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha(\Theta', \textbf{p}', \Theta, \textbf{p}) = \text{min}\left( 1, \frac{\mathcal{P}(\Theta', \textbf{p}')}{\mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p})} \right) = \text{min}\left( 1, \exp(-\mathcal{H}(\Theta', \textbf{p}') + \mathcal{H}(\Theta, \textbf{p})) \right)
\end{equation}

In exact Hamiltonian dynamics, $\mathcal{H}$ is conserved so the expected acceptance rate is unity but due to numerical errors we expect to see a lower acceptance rate. Lastly, 
we marginalize over the momenta to obtain the final posterior on our parameters, for us that will be the initial conditions. \\

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(\Theta) = \int \mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) \text{d} \textbf{p}
\end{equation}




\noindent \textbf{\underline{Gibbs sampler}} \\
Sampling additional parameters alongside the initial conditions is done using the Gibbs sampler in BORG. Suppose we have a set of $n$ parameters to sample 
${X_1, X_2, X_3, ..., X_n}$ grouped in some way given a dataset $d$. For example one group may be the initial conditions, one may be the cosmological parameters and one may be other model parameters. 
Then the desired distribution of these parameters is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(X_1, X_2, X_3, ..., X_n | d)
\end{equation}
The Gibbs scheme works by sampling each of these parameters individually rather than jointly from a complicated distribution. The scheme starts from an initial guess of the parameters 
${X_1^t, X_2^t, X_3^t, ..., X_n^t}$ at an instance $t$. The parameters are then sampled in $n$ steps as:
\begin{equation}
    X_1^{t+1} \sim \mathcal{P}(X_1^{t+1} | X_2^t, X_3^t, ..., X_n^t, d)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    X_2^{t+1} \sim \mathcal{P}(X_2^{t+1} | X_1^{t+1}, X_3^t, ..., X_n^t, d)
\end{equation}
up to:
\begin{equation}
    X_n^{t+1} \sim \mathcal{P}(X_n^{t+1} | X_1^{t+1}, X_2^{t+1}, ..., X_{n-1}^{t+1}, d)
\end{equation}
This is then repeated until a converged state is reached giving us the desired distribution. \\

\subsection{Forward Model}
\subsubsection{Particle Mass Assignment} \label{sec:cic}

The initial density field $\delta^{IC}$ is drawn from a Gaussian prior and represented as a set of particles distributed on a grid. 
BORG relies on a Cloud In Cell (CIC) mass assignment on the grid. This mass assignment 
works by assigning a "cloud" to each particle and distributing its mass to the neighbouring grid points according to the overlap between the cloud and these points. 
The assignment is done in the following way: Each particle is treated as a uniform-density cube having a mass of unity. Each particle overlaps with up to $2^3=8$ neighbouring grid cells. 
The fraction of the particle's mass assigned to each cell is proportional to the volume overlap between the particle cube and the grid cell. Let us consider a 3D grid 
with a cell size of $\Delta x$ and a particle at the position $X_p = (x_p, y_p, z_p)$ lies within a cell whose lower-left corner is a grid point $(i, j, k)$ where: 
\begin{equation}
    i = \biggl \lfloor \frac{x_p}{\Delta x} \biggr \rfloor, \quad j = \biggl \lfloor \frac{y_p}{\Delta x} \biggr \rfloor , \quad k = \biggl \lfloor \frac{z_p}{\Delta x} \biggr \rfloor
\end{equation}
We define the fractional distance as:
\begin{equation}
    d_x = \frac{x_p}{\Delta x} - i, \quad d_y = \frac{y_p}{\Delta x} - j, \quad d_z = \frac{z_p}{\Delta x} - k
\end{equation} 

So that $d_{x, y, z} \in [0,1[$. The particle's mass $m_p$ is distributed to the 8 surrounding nodes $(i+a, j+b, k+c)$ with $a,b,c \in [0,1[$ : 
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{i+a, j+b, k+c} += \frac{m_p}{\Delta x ^ 3} . T_x ^{(a)} . T_y ^{(b)} . T_z ^{(c)}
\end{equation}
with $T_{x, y, z}^{0} = 1 - d_{x, y, z}$ and $T_{x, y, z}^{1} = d_{x, y, z}$. 
There are other mass assignment schemes, however the choice of CIC reflects a balance between accuracy and computational cost (\cite{Hockney_1988}). 
The Nearest Grid Point (NGP) scheme assigns the entire particle mass to the closest grid node and is computationally inexpensive, but introduces large truncation errors. 
Conversely, higher-order kernels such as the Triangular-Shaped Cloud (TSC) and Piecewise Cubic Spline (PCS) schemes distribute the particle mass over progressively 
larger interpolation kernels producing smoother, more accurate density fields at the expense 
of spreading each particle's mass over $3^3$ or $4^3$ cells rather than the $2^3 = 8$ nodes used by CIC. \\

\subsubsection{Gravity Model} \label{sec:gravity_model}
Simply evolving the $\delta^{IC}$ field using the equations of motion would be too computationally expensive since the number of particles in our field can go up to $10^9$ 
particles. Developments in the implementation of a gravity model that is accurate and fast have been a very important topic in the development of BORG. I present the 
main models that are used in BORG. Additionally, the movements of the particles in the density field directly gives us access to the underlying velocity field. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{LPT}} \\

The first gravity model is the Lagrangian Perturbation Theory or LPT; it is also known as the Zeldovich approximation (\cite{zeldovich_1970}, \cite{Crocce_2006}). 
The idea is to expand the density field pertubatively $\delta(x, t) = \delta^{(1)}(x, t) + \delta^{(2)}(x, t) + ... $, which is the Eulerian expansion, as well 
as the displacement field $\Psi(q, t)= \Psi^{(1)}(q, t) + \Psi^{(2)}(q, t) + ...$ which maps the initial positions $q$ (Lagrangian coordinates) to the final positions $x$ (Eulerian coordinates). 
In comoving coordinates, the equations of motion for dark matter particles are: 
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{x} + 2H\dot{x} = -\frac{1}{a^2} \nabla_x \phi
\end{equation}
where $H = \dot{a}/a$ is the Hubble parameter, $a$ the scale factor and $\phi$ is the gravitational potential satisfying the Poisson equation: 
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2_x \phi = 4\pi G \bar{\rho} a^2 \delta(x,t)
\end{equation}

With the position being: 
\begin{equation}
    x(q,t) = q + \Psi(q,t)
    \label{eqn:eqn_of_motion_general}
\end{equation}
This means we can rewrite the equations of motion as: 
\begin{equation}
     \ddot{\Psi} + 2H \dot{\Psi} = -\frac{1}{a^2} \nabla_x \phi
\end{equation}
To ensure mass conservation, the continuity equation in the Lagrangian formalism gives us:  
\begin{equation}
    1+\delta(x, t) = \frac{1}{|\text{det}(J)|}
\end{equation} 
With $J$ being the Jacobian that maps $q$ to $x$. 
\begin{equation}
    J_{ij} = \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial q_j} = \delta_{ij} + \frac{\partial \Psi_i}{\partial q_j}
\end{equation}

At first order, we can expand the determinant $\text{det}(J) \simeq 1 + \nabla_q . \Psi^{(1)}$ giving the first term in the expansion of the density field as: 
\begin{equation}
    \delta^{(1)}(x,t) \simeq - \nabla_q . \Psi^{(1)}
\end{equation}

If we continue the demonstration, we can obtain the first order approximation for the position of the density field particles at a time $t$ 
and its velocity as: 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{x(q, t) = q - D_1(t) \nabla_q \phi^{(1)}(q)}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{v(q, t) = -a H f D_1(t) \nabla_q \phi^{(1)}(q)}
\end{equation}
Where $D_1$ is the linear growth factor, defined earlier in Chapter 1 as $D_+$, and $f=d \ln D_1 / d \ln a$ the linear growth rate of structures (seen in Chapter 1 Section \ref{sec:newton}). 
It is important to note that the first-order approximation becomes 
increasingly inaccurate as density fluctuations grow and structure formation 
enters the non-linear regime. In this regime, the higher-order terms in the 
perturbative expansion of the displacement field become increasingly 
important, particularly for the formation of dense structures such as halos. 
The first-order approximation therefore provides a good description of 
structure formation on large and mildly non-linear scales, but becomes 
inaccurate on smaller scales. Going to higher orders such as 2LPT (2nd order approximation) delays this breakdown but does not prevent it. Physically, 
it can be shown that this occurs around $k\approx10^{-1} h \text{Mpc}^{-1}$ at $z=0$ \cite{Chan_2014}. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{2LPT}} \\

We can also use the second order approximation of the Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (2LPT \cite{Bouchet_1994}) as our gravity solver. That means the displacement field is written as 
$\Psi(q,t) = \Psi^{(1)}(q, t) + \Psi^{(2)}(q, t)$ and one can demonstrate that the second order term of the displacement is: 

\begin{equation}
    \Psi^{(2)}(q,t) = -D_2 (t) \nabla_q \phi^{(2)} (q)
\end{equation}
where $D_2(t)$ is the second order growth factor. We also get the following Poisson equation for the gravitational potential: 
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_q^{2} \phi^{(2)} (q) = \sum_{i>j} \left( \frac{\partial^{2} \phi^{(1)} }{\partial^{2}x_i} \frac{\partial^{2} \phi^{(1)} }{\partial^{2}x_j} - \left[ \frac{\partial^{2} \phi^{(1)} }{\partial x_i x_j} \right]^2 \right)
\end{equation}

The final position and velocity of the density field in 2LPT are: 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{x(q,t) = q - D_1(t) \nabla_q \phi^{(1)} - D_2(t) \nabla_q \phi^{(2)}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{v(q,t) = -aH \left[ f_1 D_1 \nabla_q \phi ^{(1)} + f_2 D_2 \nabla_q \phi ^{(2)} \right]}
\end{equation}
where $f_1$ is the linear growth factor $f_1=d \ln D_1 / d \ln a$ and $f_2 = d \ln D_2 / d \ln a$. 

\noindent \textbf{\underline{PM}} \\

The goal of the Particle Mesh (PM) gravity model is to evolve the particles in the simulation after having done a mass assignment, like CIC (Section \ref{sec:cic}). 
The use of a grid and Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) 
to solve the Poisson equation reduces the computational complexity of 
calculating the gravitational forces from $\sim\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ for direct 
interactions between particles to $\sim\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$.  
The equations of motion and the Poisson are adapted to be solved on the BORG grid chosen, they are given by Equation \ref{eqn:eqn_of_motion_general} and 
$ \frac{\text{d}^2 \Psi(q, a)}{\text{d} a^2} = - \nabla_x \phi(x, a) $. The procedure is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mass assignment, like CIC, to obtain the density field $\delta$.
    \item Transform to Fourier space $\delta(x) \rightarrow \tilde{\delta}(k)$ using a Fast Fourier Transform.
    \item Solve the Poisson equation in Fourier space.
    \item Compute the force field, or the gravitational acceleration, in Fourier space $\tilde{g}(k)$ then transform it to Cartesian space to get $g(x)$.
    \item Interpolate the forces back to particles.
    \item Time integration to get the next position and momentum of the particles.
\end{itemize} 

The PM gravity model is accurate up to the resolution of the grid, which is the most accurate gravity model we can use in BORG at the current moment. 
It therefore 
provides a more accurate description of the density and velocity fields down 
to the scale resolved by the mesh (\cite{Feng_2016}). However, reaching this accuracy requires 
repeating the full PM cycle at least 100 times (\cite{Tassev_2013}) which will incur a large computational cost. If we decide to reduce the steps in PM we would lose accuracy on all scales.\\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{COLA}} \\

The last model I will present is COmoving Lagrangian Acceleration or COLA (\cite{Tassev_2013}). The goal of COLA is to be able to solve the equations of motion and the Poisson equation for 
particles that undergo trajectories in LPT. This method allows us to trade accuracy at small scales to gain computational speed while maintaining accuracy at large scales. 
This is done by decomposing the displacement field into two components: 
\begin{equation}
    \Psi(q, a) = \Psi^{LPT}(q, a) + \Psi_{res}^{COLA}(q,a)
\end{equation}
where $\Psi^{LPT}(q, a)$ is the LPT displacement field and $\Psi_{res}^{COLA}(q,a)$ the residual displacement of each particle as observed from a frame comoving 
with an "LPT observer" whose trajectory is defined by $\Psi^{LPT}$. If we temporarily omit the Hubble expansion, we get the following equations of motion: 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}^2 \Psi(q, a)}{\text{d} a^2} = - \nabla_x \phi(x, a)
\end{equation}
This can be rewritten in the comoving frame of the "LPT" observer as:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\frac{\text{d}^2 \Psi_{res}^{COLA}(q,a)}{\text{d} a^2} = - \nabla_x \phi(x, a) - \frac{\text{d}^2 \Psi^{LPT}(q,a)}{\text{d} a^2}}
\end{equation}
We can think of the LPT as an additional force acting on the particles due to the use of a non-inertial frame of reference. LPT also experiences lower 
typical accelerations compared to the natural cosmological frame. Numerically, this produces equivalent accuracy to solving the full equations of motion while 
using fewer steps (\cite{Tassev_2013}). The expected number of steps in COLA is around $10$ unlike PM. 
A visual representation of what COLA does can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:cola_schematic}. \\

    \caption{Schematic representing how COLA works found in \cite{Bartlett_2025}. The orange line represents the motion of the particle from the LPT frame of reference and the blue lines are the residuals COLA calculates from the true position to correct for it.}
    \label{fig:cola_schematic}
\end{figure}

If we denote $x(a) = x_{LPT}(a) + x_{COLA}(a)$ and the momentum $p = p_{LPT} + p_{COLA}$ the equations to solve then become : 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}a} =  \mathcal{D}(a) p_{COLA} - \frac{\text{d}D_1}{\text{d}\Psi^{(1)}} +  \frac{\text{d}D_2}{\text{d}\Psi^{(2)}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}p_{COLA}}{\text{d}a} = \mathcal{K}(a) \left[ \nabla(\Delta^{-1}\delta) - \mathcal{V}[D_1](a)\Psi^{(1)} + \mathcal{V}[D_2](a) \Psi^{(2)} \right]
\end{equation}

with $\mathcal{D}(a) = \frac{f(a)}{a^2}$, also called the "drift" prefactor, and $\mathcal{K}(a) = -\frac{3}{2} \Omega_m H_0^2 f(a)/a$, also called the "kick" prefactor. 
These terms come from the kick-drift-kick (KDK) algorithm that is used to update the particle positions. The kick phase boosts the momentum of the particles while the drift phase changes 
their positions. The KDK scheme goes as follows : At an instant $n$, positions and momenta at an instant $n+1$ are determined in the following way: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item Kick $p^{COLA}_{n+1/2} = p^{COLA}_{n} + F^{COLA}_{n}.\frac{\Delta a}{2}$
    \item Drift $x_{n+1} = x_n + p^{COLA}_{n+1/2}. \delta a$
    \item Kick $p^{COLA}_{n+1} = p^{COLA}_{n+1/2} + F^{COLA}_{n+1}.\frac{\Delta a}{2}$
\end{itemize}
where at each step, the force $F^{COLA}$ is computed using a PM solver from above. \\


We can look at a visual difference between the 2LPT gravity solver, COLA and what a "true" density field looks like which is a N-body simulation named GADGET (\cite{Springel_2005}, \cite{Springel_2021}) in Figure \ref{fig:cola_nbody_2lpt}. 
The 2LPT results are obtained in ~3 timesteps, the COLA results in ~10 timesteps and GADGET results are obtained with ~200 timesteps. 
As expected, the COLA results manage to capture a lot of the details found on small scales as opposed to the 2LPT results while requiring far fewer timesteps than a full N-body simulation.

\subsubsection{Connecting the Dark Matter Field to Observables} \label{sec:observable_to_dm}

The forward model gives us access to the dark matter field of the Universe today. 
However in practice we do not directly observe this field to compare it with the output from the forward model. 
We therefore need to introduce means to relate the dark matter field to observables.

Galaxies are the most abundant observables for cosmological analyses. The way galaxies trace dark matter has been a central question in modern cosmology as it intervenes 
in many cosmological analyses. Relating the galaxy field $\delta_g$ to the matter field $\delta_m$ requires the introduction of a galaxy bias model. 
An incorrect bias model can bias the inference of cosmological parameters as they degenerate. One simple way 
to see this would be with the linear bias model (\cite{Kaiser_1984}). The model assumes a linear relation between the galaxy and dark matter densities which is written as:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_g(\mathbf{r}) = b \delta_m(\mathbf{r})
\end{equation}
with $b$ a scalar. If we would write down the power spectrum of the galaxy field we get:
\begin{equation}
    P_{gg}(k) = b^2 P_{mm}(k)
\end{equation}
From Equation \ref{eqn:pk_lin}, we know that that the amplitude of the power spectrum depends on $A_s$ or $\sigma_8$. We see that the galaxy power spectrum's amplitude also 
depends on $b$, rendering both parameters degenerate when trying to fit the power spectrum. Other examples of bias models include the 
nonlinear local bias (\cite{Fry_1993}), which is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_g = \sum_{i=1} b_i \frac{\delta_m^i}{i!}
\end{equation}
This model contains multiple bias parameters relating both densities at different orders. 
More complex models exist such as the Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure bias (EFTofLSS) (\cite{Mirbabayi_2015}). 
This approach has been used in both standard galaxy clustering analyses based on the two-point statistics as well as in field-level inference based on EFT (\cite{Schmidt_2021}).
The model is written as:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_g = \sum_i b_{\mathcal{O}_i}\mathcal{O}_i
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}_i$ are operators that are made of $\delta_m$, its derivatives, tidal field and shear. 

For a field level inference with BORG, the galaxy bias model is, so far, an empirical expression motivated by hydrodynamical simulations. 
For BORG's latest data products Manticore-Local and Manticore-Deep (\cite{Mcalpine_2025,Mcalpine_2026}) 
the bias model is expressed as a sigmoid-truncated double 
power law which follows:
\begin{equation}
    b(\rho) = \frac{\rho^{\alpha}}{1+\exp \left[ \left( \gamma_t - \ln \rho \right)/\gamma_s \right]}. \left( 1 + \exp \left[ \beta \left( \ln \rho - \beta_0 \right)\right] \right)^{-\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\rho = 1 + \delta^F$, $\gamma_t$ defines the transition point between the truncation and the first power-law regime, $\gamma_s$ controls the steepness of the 
transition, $\alpha$ is the first power-law exponent, $\beta$ defines the slope of the second power-law component and $\beta_0$ sets the transition scale between the two power-law regimes. 
The sigmoid truncation regulates galaxy formation at low densities. The first power law represents the dominant relationship between the dark matter density and galaxy 
formation. Finally the second power law allows for a gradual transition at higher densities. 

The ingredients introduced so far define the generative model linking the density field to the observed galaxy distribution. 
The Bayesian inference is completed by specifying a likelihood for the observations and a prior. 
Traditional approaches assume the galaxy field to follow a Poisson sampling process within each voxel, but recent studies (\cite{Ata_2015, Gruen_2018, Britt_2024}) have observed super-Poissonian behavior (variance > mean) 
in the scatter of galaxy counts, particularly in high-density regions. For Manticore-Local and Manticore-Deep, a Generalized Poisson likelihood was introduced to regulate the 
galaxy count variance independently from the mean in galaxy subcatalogues. The likelihood is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi (N|\lambda, B) = \frac{\lambda(1-B)}{N!} \left[ \lambda(1-B) + NB  \right]^{N-1}\exp[-\lambda(1-B) - NB]
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the Poisson intensity, $N$ is the observed number of galaxies, and $B \in [0,1]$ is the overdispersion parameter, 
which governs the deviation from the standard Poisson variance. When $B=0$, the standard Poisson case is recovered. To reflect the observed behavior of higher-density 
regions exhibiting greater variance in galaxy count, the overdispersion parameter is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    B(\rho, B_{\alpha}, B_{\beta}) = B_{\alpha} \rho ^{B_{\beta}}
\end{equation}
where $\rho = 1+\delta^F$ is the dark matter density, $B_{\alpha}$ a normalization parameter and $B_{\beta}$ determines the slope of the power-law scaling. 

In this thesis, however, our objective is not to infer the density field from galaxy clustering, but from peculiar velocity tracers, namely Type Ia supernovae. 
This requires replacing the galaxy count likelihood and the associated galaxy bias model with models appropriate for peculiar velocity observations. 
These modifications constitute the main methodological developments of this work and are presented in the following chapter.
