\section{Towards a next generation of field-level inferences}
Although field-level inferences present great potential in the future of cosmological analyses, there are ongoing efforts to imrpove some of its aspects its practical 
implementation. 

\subsection{Gravity solvers}

Gravity solvers such as LPT, COLA, and PM are non-linear approximations of the full equations of motion derived from the GR 
framework. They are used because solving the exact equations of motion would be computationally too expensive, rendering Bayesian sampling of the 
initial conditions intractable. The closest approximation to the full solution is the PM model, which solves the equations of motion on a 
discretised grid. Recent efforts have focused on developing faster and more accurate gravity solvers to improve upon these approximations. One 
example is JaxPM\footnote{\url{https://github.com/DifferentiableUniverseInitiative/JaxPM}}, which accelerates the PM gravity solver by parallelizing computational 
tasks on GPUs. The solver is also fully differentiable, as a natural consequence of its JAX implementation.

Another development is the introduction of a machine learning-based field-level emulator within BORG by \cite{Doeser_2024}, which completely 
replaces the gravity solver. The initial conditions are first evolved with LPT and then corrected by a differentiable emulator trained on 
N-body simulations, following the scheme:
\begin{equation}
    \delta^{IC} \longrightarrow \delta^{LPT} \longrightarrow 
    \delta^F_{\text{emulated}} \approx \delta^F_{\text{true}}
\end{equation}
This provides a fast method to obtain an evolved density field that accounts for non-linearities beyond the reach of perturbation theory. 
However, since the N-body solver is entirely replaced by the emulator, any emulation errors cannot be corrected by design.

A complementary approach is the COmoving Computer Acceleration framework (COCA, \cite{Bartlett_2025}), a hybrid model that combines a machine 
learning emulator with a physical N-body solver. Rather than replacing the gravity solver entirely, COCA uses a neural network to predict 
particle trajectories in an emulated frame of reference, after which the equations of motion are solved for the residual displacement in this 
frame. Since LPT already provides a good approximation to the particle trajectories, the emulator is trained on the displacement relative to 
LPT, reducing the magnitude of the residual that the physical solver must correct for. The total displacement can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi(\mathbf{q}, a) = \Psi^{LPT}(\mathbf{q}, a) + \Psi^{\text{ML}}(\mathbf{q}, a)  + \Psi_{res}^{COCA}(\mathbf{q}, a)
\end{equation}
where $\Psi^{\text{ML}}(\mathbf{q}, a)$ is the displacement predicted by the emulator and $\Psi_{res}^{COCA}(\mathbf{q}, a)$ 
is the residual displacement, which is smaller than the corresponding residual in COLA. Crucially, because the physical equations of motion are 
still solved in the emulated frame, any emulation error is corrected by design. This 
guarantees convergence to the true solution as the number of force evaluations increases, while requiring fewer evaluations than a full 
N-body simulation. The differences between COLA and COCA are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:cola_vs_coca}.

\subsection{Galaxy bias models}

Galaxies are the most abundent observables for cosmological analyses. The way galaxies trace dark matter has been a central question in modern cosmology as it intervenes 
in many cosmological analyses. Relating the galaxy field $\delta_g$ to the matter field $\delta_m$ requires the introduction of a galaxy bias model. The need for a bias 
model is not only useful for a field-level inference however an incorrect bias model can bias the inference of cosmological parameters as they degenerated. One simple way 
to see this would be with the linear bias model (\cite{Kaiser_1984}). The model assumes a linear relation between the galaxy and dark matter densities which is written as:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_g(\mathbf{r}) = b \delta_m(\mathbf{r})
\end{equation}
with a $b$ a scalar. If we would write down the power spectrum of the galaxy field we get:
\begin{equation}
    P_{gg}(k) = b^2 P_{mm}(k)
\end{equation}
From Equation \ref{eqn:pk_lin}, we know that that the amplitude of the power spectrum depends on $A_s$ or $\sigma_8$. We see that the galaxy power spectrum's amplitude also 
depends on $b$, rendering both parameters degenerate when trying to fit the power spectrum. Other examples of bias models include the 
nonlinear local bias (\cite{Fry_1993}), which is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_g = \sum_{i=1} b_i \frac{\delta_m^i}{i!}
\end{equation}
This model contains multiple bias parameters related both densities at different orders. 
More complex models exist such as the Effective Field Theory of Large Scale Structure bias (EFTofLSS) (\cite{Mirbabayi_2015}). The model is written as:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_g = \sum_i b_{\mathcal{O}_i}\mathcal{O}_i
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}_i$ are operators that are made of $\delta_m$, its derivatives, tidal field and shear. 

For field-level inference, we can look at the bias model used in the Manticore-local Digital Twin (\cite{Mcalpine_2025}). The bias model is expressed as a sigmoid-truncated double 
power law which follows:
\begin{equation}
    b(\rho) = \frac{\rho^{\alpha}}{1+\exp \left[ \left( \gamma_t - \ln \rho \right)/\gamma_s \right]}. \left( 1 + \exp \left[ \beta \left( \ln \rho - \beta_0 \right)\right] \right)^{-\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\rho = 1 + \delta_m$, $\gamma_t$ defines the transition point between the truncation and the first power-law regime, $\gamma_s$ controls the steepness of the 
transition, $\alpha$ is the first power-law exponent, $\beta$ defines the slope of the second power-law component and $\beta_0$ sets the transition scale between the two power-law regimes. 
The sigmoid truncation, regulates galaxy formation at low densities. The first power law represents the dominant relationship between the dark matter density and galaxy 
formation. Finally the second power law allows for a gradual transition at higher densities. \\

\subsection{Parameter sampling}

As we have mentioned previously, the number of parameters sampled in a field-level inference can reach very large numbers ($\approx 10^9$) which can take a lot of computational 
time to determine. Therefore, efforts to reduce the computational expenses of the parameter sampling have been ongoing in the context of field-level inferences. Different samplers 
have been tested in \cite{Simon_2025}: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), same as BORG, No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) with and without Gibbs scheme, an adjusted microcanonical 
sampler (MAMS) and an unadjusted microcanonical sampler (MCLMC). 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HMC:} as described in Section \ref{sec:borg_bayes}, it treats the parameter space as the positions of the particles following the classical equations of motion. 
    The final state is used as a proposal for the Metropolis-Hastings adjustement step after which the dynamics are repeated.
    \item \textbf{NUTS:} is a variant of HMC that automatically and dynamically adapts the trajectory. It adds sets of proposals along the 
    trajectories, which are stopped whenever they start to reverse direction, thereby avoiding redundant exploration and the need for manual tuning.
    \item \textbf{MAMS:} Metropolis-Adjusted Microcanonical Sampler is a microcanonical counterpart to the canonical HMC. Instead of sampling the canonical ensemble of HMC $\mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) \propto \exp(-\mathcal{H}(\Theta, \textbf{p}))$, 
    the sampling is done on a fixed energy level $\mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) \propto \delta(\mathcal{H}(\Theta, \textbf{p})- E)$ which avoids momentum resampling and random 
    jumps. Just like with HMC, the final state is used as a proposal for the Metropolis-Hastings adjustement step after which the dynamics are repeated.
    \item \textbf{MCLM:} MicroCanonical Langevin Monte Carlo also samples from the microcanonical ensemble as MAMS but with the difference of refreshing the momentum at each steps. 
    This method allows the parameters to properly explore the entirety of the exploration space, i.e. ensure ergodicity. Unlike MAMS however, this sampler does not include 
    a Metropolis-Hastings adjustement step, meaning any biases introduced will have to be corrected at the level of the integration step.
\end{itemize} 

Results of the number of steps required per effective sample for each sampler is displayed in Figure \ref{fig:hugo_figure}. What the paper finds is that MCLMC outperforms all other samplers 
achieving the same results with almost ten times less step. \\

Another approach to improve the speed of parameter sampling involves the use of trained sample generation set neural networks to directly infer the posteriors of sets of parameters. 
These models are called diffusion models. These models are trained on many N-body simulations which is computationally expensive. However once the models have been generated, 
their use is direct. An example of the application of a diffusion model can be found in \cite{Mudur_2023}. The diffusion model used here was trained to learn the 
characteristics of a density given a set of cosmological parameters $(\Omega_m, \sigma_8)$. This was then used to infer the $(\Omega_m, \sigma_8)$ directly from the simulated fields. 
Their results show that the diffusion model manages to sample correct parameters from the density field. We show their results in Figure (). This however emphasises the need

\subsection{Scalability}

Current and future generations of field-level inferences will have to deal with ever growing datasets covering larger volumes. This will daramatically increases the computational 
complexity needed to conduct a field-level inference. Developments in order to adapt to this scalability has been an important pillar of in the construction of the next generation 
of field-level inferences. The methods and models introduced the last sections all contribute towards accelerating the computational speed but not all address the computational cost 
and memory usage. One of the key methods that directly deals with this has been applied in Manticore-local (\cite{Mcalpine_2025}) and Manticore-deep (\cite{Mcalpine_2026}) which is tiling. 
Infering the density field of Manticore-deep is done on a $\left(4096 h^{-1} \text{Mpc}\right)^3$ volume with a resolution of $\left(4 h^{-1} \text{Mpc}\right)^3$ which 
requires $1024^3$ parameters on the grid to be inferred, which exceeds the current field-level memory usage and runtime available. To overcome that, they separate the full volume into 
$\left(1024 h^{-1} \text{Mpc}\right)^3$ subvolumes that would be inferred separately on a $256^3$. 
The observational footprint of the surveys used in Manticore-deep covers only a subset of the full volume, with 27 subvolumes containing galaxy constraints. 
The remaining subvolumes, which do not overlap with the survey region, are populated with independent Gaussian white-noise realizations drawn from the $\Lambda$CDM prior 
for each posterior sample. 
To minimize artificial boundary effects resulting from the periodic boundary conditions imposed within individual tiles, neighbouring subvolumes are connected through 
"buffer" regions. The outer half of each buffer is excluded from the likelihood and evolves solely according to the prior, thereby absorbing 
boundary effects, while the inner half provides an overlap region where galaxies near tile boundaries contribute to multiple neighbouring inferences to keep continuity 
when individual posteriors are combined. 
Following inference, the buffer regions are removed, leaving voxel cores containing only the reconstructed signal. These cores are then assembled into the full volume, 
while the remaining unconstrained regions are completed using independent $\Lambda$CDM prior realizations. This produces a full initial condition realization spanning the entire 
$\left(4096 h^{-1} \text{Mpc}\right)^3$ volume that remains consistent with the assumed cosmology while preserving the observationally constrained structures. \\
