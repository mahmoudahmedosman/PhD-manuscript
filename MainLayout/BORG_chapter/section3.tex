\subsection{Gravity solvers}

Gravity solvers such as LPT, COLA, and PM are non-linear approximations of the full equations of motion derived from the GR 
framework. They are used because solving the exact equations of motion would be computationally too expensive, rendering Bayesian sampling of the 
initial conditions intractable. The closest approximation to the full solution is the PM model, which solves the equations of motion on a 
discretised grid. Recent efforts have focused on developing faster and more accurate gravity solvers to improve upon these approximations. One 
example is JaxPM\footnote{\url{https://github.com/DifferentiableUniverseInitiative/JaxPM}}, which accelerates the PM gravity solver by parallelizing computational 
tasks on GPUs. The solver is also fully differentiable, as a natural consequence of its JAX implementation.

Another development is the introduction of a machine learning-based field-level emulator within BORG by \cite{Doeser_2024}, which completely 
replaces the gravity solver. The initial conditions are first evolved with LPT and then corrected by a differentiable emulator trained on 
N-body simulations, following the scheme:
\begin{equation}
    \delta^{IC} \longrightarrow \delta^{LPT} \longrightarrow 
    \delta^F_{\text{emulated}} \approx \delta^F_{\text{true}}
\end{equation}
This provides a fast method to obtain an evolved density field that accounts for non-linearities beyond the reach of perturbation theory. 
However, since the N-body solver is entirely replaced by the emulator, any emulation errors cannot be corrected by design.

A complementary approach is the COmoving Computer Acceleration framework (COCA, \cite{Bartlett_2025}), a hybrid model that combines a machine 
learning emulator with a physical N-body solver. Rather than replacing the gravity solver entirely, COCA uses a neural network to predict 
particle trajectories in an emulated frame of reference, after which the equations of motion are solved for the residual displacement in this 
frame. Since LPT already provides a good approximation to the particle trajectories, the emulator is trained on the displacement relative to 
LPT, reducing the magnitude of the residual that the physical solver must correct for. The total displacement can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi(\mathbf{q}, a) = \Psi^{LPT}(\mathbf{q}, a) + \Psi^{\text{ML}}(\mathbf{q}, a)  + \Psi_{res}^{COCA}(\mathbf{q}, a)
\end{equation}
where $\Psi^{\text{ML}}(\mathbf{q}, a)$ is the displacement predicted by the emulator and $\Psi_{res}^{COCA}(\mathbf{q}, a)$ 
is the residual displacement, which is smaller than the corresponding residual in COLA. Crucially, because the physical equations of motion are 
still solved in the emulated frame, any emulation error is corrected by design. This 
guarantees convergence to the true solution as the number of force evaluations increases, while requiring fewer evaluations than a full 
N-body simulation. The differences between COLA and COCA are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:cola_vs_coca}.
