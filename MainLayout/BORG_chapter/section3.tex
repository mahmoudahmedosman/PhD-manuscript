\section{Towards a Next Generation of Field-level Inferences}
Although field-level inferences present great potential in cosmological analyses, there are ongoing efforts to improve several aspects of their practical 
implementation. 

\subsection{Gravity Solvers}

Gravity solvers such as LPT, COLA, and PM are non-linear approximations of the full equations of motion derived from the GR 
framework. They are used because solving the exact equations of motion would be computationally too expensive, rendering Bayesian sampling of the 
initial conditions intractable. The closest approximation to the full solution is the PM model in field-level inference, which solves the equations of motion on a 
discretised grid. Recent efforts have focused on developing faster and more accurate gravity solvers to improve upon these approximations. One 
example is JaxPM\footnote{\url{https://github.com/DifferentiableUniverseInitiative/JaxPM}}, which accelerates the PM gravity solver by parallelizing computational 
tasks on GPUs. The solver is also fully differentiable, as a natural consequence of its JAX implementation. Another example of an accelerated PM gravity solver is 
FastPM (\cite{Feng_2016}) which is also differentiable. 
More recently, BullFrog (\cite{Rampf_2025}) has been developed as an 
alternative gravity solver that combines perturbative calculations with 
efficient time integration to provide an accurate and computationally 
efficient approximation to full $N$-body evolution. 


Another development is the introduction of a machine learning-based field-level emulator within BORG by \cite{Doeser_2024}, which completely 
replaces the gravity solver. The initial conditions are first evolved with LPT and then corrected by a differentiable emulator trained on 
N-body simulations, following the scheme:
\begin{equation}
    \delta^{IC} \longrightarrow \delta^{LPT} \longrightarrow 
    \delta^F_{\text{emulated}} \approx \delta^F_{\text{true}}
\end{equation}
This provides a fast method to obtain an evolved density field that accounts for non-linearities beyond the reach of perturbation theory. 
However, since the N-body solver is entirely replaced by the emulator, any emulation errors cannot be corrected by design. 
This can call into question the reliability of the inferred field-level if not properly accounted for. 

A complementary approach is the COmoving Computer Acceleration framework (COCA, \cite{Bartlett_2025}), a hybrid model that combines a machine 
learning emulator with a physical N-body solver. Rather than replacing the gravity solver entirely, COCA uses a neural network to predict 
particle trajectories in an emulated frame of reference, after which the equations of motion are solved for the residual displacement in this 
frame. Since LPT already provides a good approximation to the particle trajectories on large scales, the emulator is trained on the displacement relative to 
LPT, reducing the magnitude of the residual that the physical solver must correct for. The total displacement can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi(\mathbf{q}, a) = \Psi^{LPT}(\mathbf{q}, a) + \Psi^{\text{ML}}(\mathbf{q}, a)  + \Psi_{res}^{COCA}(\mathbf{q}, a)
\end{equation}
where $\Psi^{\text{ML}}(\mathbf{q}, a)$ is the displacement predicted by the emulator and $\Psi_{res}^{COCA}(\mathbf{q}, a)$ 
is the residual displacement, which is smaller than the corresponding residual in COLA. 
Therefore fewer steps are required to reach a converged density field. 
Crucially, because the physical equations of motion are 
still solved in the emulated frame, any emulation error is corrected by design. This 
guarantees convergence to the true solution as the number of force evaluations increases, while requiring fewer evaluations than a full 
N-body simulation. The differences between COLA and COCA are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:cola_vs_coca}.

\subsection{Galaxy Bias Models}

While the bias models presented in Section \ref{sec:observable_to_dm} provide an effective description of the connection between the matter and galaxy fields, 
improving this relation remains an active area of research. 
\cite{Bartlett_2024} demonstrated the limitations of local-in-matter-density 
(LIMD) bias models using a model-independent permutation test on the Quijote 
N-body simulations (\cite{Villaescusa_2020}). By permuting halo counts between 
voxels of approximately equal dark matter density, they showed that if LIMD 
biasing were valid, the statistical properties of the permuted and unpermuted 
fields would be indistinguishable. Instead, they found significant scale-dependent 
deviations in the halo power spectrum, with more than 25\% excess power on the 
largest scales probed, for halos less massive than $\sim 10^{15} \, h^{-1} 
M_{\odot}$ and voxel sizes below $\sim 30 \, h^{-1}$ Mpc. This demonstrates 
that halo formation depends on non-local information beyond the local matter 
density, and that no LIMD bias model, however complex, can reproduce the 
distribution of halos on these scales. This motivates the development of more 
expressive bias models, such as EFT-based bias expansions (\cite{Schmidt_2021}).

New approaches based on neural networks aim to improve the accuracy of 
bias models while preserving the computational efficiency required for 
field-level inference. One example is the PineTree model 
(\cite{Ding_2024}), a generative differentiable halo model designed 
for galaxy surveys. The neural network learns halo abundance and 
stochasticity from dark matter density field simulations while 
incorporating environmental information beyond a purely local 
description. The model is computationally efficient, using only 17 
parameters and GPU acceleration through JAX, and has been benchmarked 
against N-body simulations run with GADGET (\cite{Springel_2021}) 
using the ROCKSTAR halo finder. PineTree accurately reproduces halo 
clustering statistics including on non-linear scales, making it 
suitable for generating halo mock catalogues for field-level inference. 
Figure \ref{fig:pinetree} compares the halo mass function, power 
spectrum, and bispectrum obtained from PineTree against the N-body 
truth.

\subsection{Parameter Sampling}

As we have mentioned previously, the number of parameters sampled in a field-level inference can reach very large numbers ($\approx 10^9$), which is computationally demanding. 
Therefore, efforts to reduce the computational expenses of the parameter sampling have been ongoing in the context of field-level inferences. Different samplers 
have been tested in \cite{Simon_2025}: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), same as BORG, No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) with and without Gibbs scheme, an adjusted microcanonical 
sampler (MAMS) and an unadjusted microcanonical sampler (MCLMC). 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HMC:} as described in Section \ref{sec:borg_sampler}, it treats the parameter space as the positions of the particles following the classical equations of motion. 
    The final state is used as a proposal for the Metropolis-Hastings adjustement step after which the dynamics are repeated.
    \item \textbf{NUTS:} is a variant of HMC that automatically and dynamically adapts the trajectory. It adds sets of proposals along the 
    trajectories, which are stopped whenever they start to reverse direction, thereby avoiding redundant exploration and the need for manual tuning.
    \item \textbf{MAMS:} Metropolis-Adjusted Microcanonical Sampler is a microcanonical counterpart of the canonical HMC. Instead of sampling the canonical ensemble of HMC $\mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) \propto \exp(-\mathcal{H}(\Theta, \textbf{p}))$, 
    the sampling is done at a fixed energy level $\mathcal{P}(\Theta, \textbf{p}) \propto \delta(\mathcal{H}(\Theta, \textbf{p})- E)$, which avoids momentum resampling and random 
    jumps. Just like with HMC, the final state is used as a proposal for the Metropolis-Hastings adjustement step after which the dynamics are repeated.
    \item \textbf{MCLMC:} MicroCanonical Langevin Monte Carlo also samples from the microcanonical ensemble as MAMS but with the difference of refreshing the momentum at each step. 
    This method allows the parameters to properly explore the entirety of the exploration space, i.e. ensure ergodicity. Unlike MAMS, however, 
    MCLMC does not include a Metropolis-Hastings correction step. Consequently, 
    the numerical errors introduced by the integration scheme are not corrected, 
    and the sampler is not guaranteed to converge to the exact target 
    distribution. In practice, the resulting bias can remain sufficiently small 
    if the integration errors are well controlled, making the method a useful 
    approximation when the computational gain from removing the 
    Metropolis-Hastings step justifies the loss of this guarantee. 
\end{itemize} 

Results of the number of steps required per effective sample for each sampler is shown in Figure \ref{fig:hugo_figure}. The paper finds that MCLMC outperforms all other samplers 
achieving the same results with nearly ten times fewer steps. This improvement comes at the cost of removing the 
Metropolis-Hastings correction, meaning that the accuracy of the resulting 
samples depends on keeping the numerical integration errors sufficiently 
small.\\

Another approach to accelerating parameter sampling is to use neural networks trained to generate samples directly from the posterior distribution. 
These models are called diffusion models (\cite{Song_2021}). These models are trained on many N-body simulations which is computationally expensive. However once the models have been generated, 
their use is direct. An example of the application of a diffusion model can be found in \cite{Mudur_2023}. The diffusion model used here was trained to learn the 
characteristics of a density given a set of cosmological parameters $(\Omega_m, \sigma_8)$. This was then used to infer the $(\Omega_m, \sigma_8)$ directly from the simulated fields. 
Their results show that the diffusion model manages to sample correct parameters from the density field. We show their results in Figure \ref{fig:diffusion_parameter}. 
This however emphasises the need to accurately reconstruct the underlying density field. \\

\subsection{Scalability}

Current and future generations of field-level inferences will have to deal with increasingly large survey volumes while maintaining sufficient spatial resolution to 
resolve small-scale structures. This poses a significant computational challenge, as the number of initial condition parameters scales with the volume for a fixed 
resolution. For example, reconstructing a $(4096\,h^{-1}\,\text{Mpc})^3$ volume at a resolution of $4\,h^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}$, as done in 
Manticore-Deep (\cite{Mcalpine_2026}), requires a $1024^3 \simeq 10^9$ dimensional initial-condition space. Future surveys such as DESI will 
cover similarly large cosmological volumes, making field-level inference at comparable spatial resolutions increasingly challenging. 
Addressing this scaling challenge has become an important pillar in the construction of next generation 
field-level inferences. 
At its core, it is mainly a question of simulating large volumes while preserving accuracy to resolve small-scale structures. 
One of the key methods that directly deals with this has been applied in Manticore-Local (\cite{Mcalpine_2025}) and Manticore-Deep (\cite{Mcalpine_2026}) which is tiling. 
Inferring the density field of Manticore-Deep is done on a $\left(4096 h^{-1} \text{Mpc}\right)^3$ volume with a resolution of $\left(4 h^{-1} \text{Mpc}\right)^3$ which 
requires $1024^3$ parameters on the grid to be inferred, which exceeds the current field-level memory usage and runtime available. To overcome that, they separate the full volume into 
$\left(1024 h^{-1} \text{Mpc}\right)^3$ subvolumes that would be inferred separately on a $256^3$ grid. 
The observational footprint of the surveys used in Manticore-Deep covers only a subset of the full volume, with 27 subvolumes actually containing galaxy data. 
The remaining subvolumes, which do not overlap with the survey region, are populated with independent Gaussian white-noise realizations drawn from the $\Lambda$CDM prior 
for each posterior sample. 
To minimize artificial boundary effects resulting from the periodic boundary conditions imposed within individual tiles, neighbouring subvolumes are connected through 
"buffer" regions. The outer half of each buffer is excluded from the likelihood and evolves solely according to the prior, thereby absorbing 
boundary effects, while the inner half provides an overlap region where galaxies near tile boundaries contribute to multiple neighbouring inferences to keep continuity 
when individual posteriors are combined. 
Following inference, the buffer regions are removed, leaving voxel cores containing only the reconstructed signal. These cores are then placed back at their corresponding positions in the full volume, reconstructing the regions constrained by the data, 
while the remaining unconstrained regions are completed using independent $\Lambda$CDM prior realizations. This produces a full initial condition realization spanning the entire 
$\left(4096 h^{-1} \text{Mpc}\right)^3$ volume that remains consistent with the assumed cosmology while preserving the observationally constrained structures. 
Figure \ref{fig:manticore_deep} presents a visual representation of the mean final density field obtained in multiple subvolumes. 
Although \cite{Mcalpine_2026} demonstrated the feasibility of reconstructing initial conditions over very large cosmological volumes using tiling, 
the application of this strategy to infer cosmological parameters jointly with the initial conditions has yet to be validated. \\

\section{Conclusion}

In this chapter we presented why a field-level inference is particularly promising for the reconstruction of peculiar velocities because it exploits the 
full spatial information and jointly reconstructs the underlying density and velocity fields. Applications of field-level inference to different 
cosmological probes have demonstrated its potential to improve the accuracy of velocity field reconstructions compared with more traditional approaches. 
This makes it particularly well suited to the measurement of the growth of structure through $f\sigma_8$.

Among the available field-level inference frameworks, we adopt BORG, which provides 
the necessary framework for jointly reconstructing the initial conditions and evolving them to the observed epoch. In BORG, the initial conditions constitute 
a high-dimensional set of parameters, from which the nonlinear density and velocity fields are obtained through a gravity solver. The posterior of thes parameters is 
explored using HMC, while additional cosmological and nuisance parameters can be sampled hierarchically using a Gibbs scheme. The 
availability of nonlinear gravity solvers within this framework further allows the reconstructed velocity field to retain information beyond the linear regime.

These elements provide the basis for the field-level reconstruction presented in this work. In the following chapter, we introduce the new supernova likelihood 
specifically developed to incorporate Type Ia supernova distance and peculiar-velocity information into the BORG framework. 
This connects the observed supernovae to the initial conditions, enabling the joint reconstruction of the underlying density and velocity fields.
