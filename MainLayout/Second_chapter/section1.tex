\section{Type Ia Supernova} \label{sec:sn_ia}

For many centuries, astronomers all over the globe would observe the emergence of a bright light in the sky thinking it was the birth of a new star in the Universe. 
In 1572, an astronomer by the name of Tycho Brahe developed an interest in such event and named the phenomenon \textit{nova stella}, latin for new star. 
His work inspired later astronomers such as Kepler and eventually led to the Copernicean revolution.\\

It wasn't until 1934 when Fritz Zwicky and Walter Baade when the term supernova (from the latin "super" and "novae" meaning new bright stars) was coined to describe the 
brightest novae observed. The supernova classifies phenomena related to the end of a star's life cycle when they go through explosions. This process is temporary therefore 
taking part in the class of \textbf{transients}. Their fluxes are initially weak and then increases over time to a point where it becomes detectable from 
the Earth, then it diminishes slowly till it disappears.\\

In the next sections we will be explaining the different classifications of supernovae as well as our understanding behind their explosion mechanisms. A particular focus 
will be on a specific class of supernovae called Type Ia and why they are heavily studied by cosmologists. \\

\subsection{Observational Classification}

Two main categories of supernovae exists: Type I and Type II each with their own subcategories of supernovae. These main categories were discovered by Rudolph Minkowski and 
Walter Baade in 1941 when studying the spectral features of 14 supernovae. Type I supernovae's absorption spectra is characterized by having no Hydrogen, contrary to Type II. 
Type I supernova are then divided in three classes (\cite{Wheeler_1985, Elias_1985}, \cite{Wheeler_1990}). Differences in the shapes of the supernova observables 
can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:sn_classification_spec}. We will go into more details on what these observables are in Section \ref{sec:sn_observables}.

\subsection{Explosion Mechanisms}

Different explosion mechanisms and progenitors exist for the different classes of supernovae. We will be separating them into two cases: core-collapse and thermonuclear.\\

\subsubsection{Core Collapse}

This explosion concerns supernovae of Types Ib, Ic and all Types II. 
At the end of a massive star’s life, successive fusion stages 
produce an iron core, whose fusion can no longer release energy. 
This core, supported mainly by electron degeneracy pressure, becomes 
unstable when electrons are captured by protons, ($e^- + p \to n + \nu_e$): the electron population decreases, the pressure drops, and 
the core becomes increasingly neutron-rich. After collapse, the core 
bounces. The shock allows the electron neutrinos produced by these 
captures to escape suddenly, generating a brief and intense 
neutronization burst, or electron-neutrino burst. The shock eventually leads to the supernova explosion. 
If the star's core has a mass inferior to the Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit ($2- 3 M_{\odot }$ \cite{Oppenheimer_1939}), 
the star will become neutron star. Larger mass stars turn to black holes. \\


\subsubsection{Thermonuclear}

Thermonuclear explosions concern Type Ia supernovae. This occurs in white dwarf stars comprised primarily of oxygen and carbon when their mass approaches 
the Chandrasekhar limit of $\sim 1.44 \, M_{\odot}$, the maximum mass supportable by 
electron degeneracy pressure (\cite{Chandrasekhar_1931}). White dwarf stars form from the remains of low mass stars (typically under $8 \, M_{\odot}$) and are dense degenerate systems. 
This term comes from the fact that the density of a white dwarf is limited by electron degeneracy pressure that comes from the Pauli exclusion principle which states that 
two electrons within the same energy levels cannot share the same quantum state. Accreting more mass from external sources induces carbon and oxygen fusion which increases 
the temperature of the white dwarf stars. If the accretion keeps on happening, the fusion of carbon and oxygen becomes a chain reaction at a constant pressure causing a 
thermonuclear explosion (\cite{Ropke_2004}). The explosion ends up releasing around $10^{51}$ erg of energy, mainly kinetic energy. Only a fraction of that is turned into luminous energy 
an at peak luminosity, the brightness of the explosion is of the same order of magnitude as its host galaxy. \\

Observations such as SN2011fe have demonstrated that Type Ia supernovae are originated from compact and dense stars, which are white dwarfs. It also important to note that 
Type Ia supernova spectra are characterized by silicon (Si II) and calcium (Ca II) absorption lines around peak luminosity. 
Type Ia supernovae luminosities present roughly a 0.4mag natural dispersion between them, which can be brought down to around 0.15mag if standardized (see Section \ref{sec:dsitance_from_flux}). 
Finally, they are observed in environments with older stars 
which is justified due to the fact that white dwarfs happen at the final stages of a star. \\

White dwarf stars are not luminous enough to be observed at large distances, therefore the progenitors of Type Ia 
supernovae have never been observed. Multiple scenarios exist to explain the explosions. We present the two main scenarios:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single degenerate} (\cite{Nomoto_1982}): This occurs when white dwarfs accrete mass from a nearby evolved companion, for example red giants or primary sequence stars. 
The accreted material, generally hydrogen, is burnt into carbon and oxygen at the surface of the white dwarf. This however sets limits to the accretion rate which narrows 
the chances of obtaining Type Ia supernovae from such systems and the inferred rate from the single degenerate scenario makes up around 10\% of the measured supernova Ia rate. 
Another argument against that scenario is that the accreted helium and hydrogen must all be transformed into carbon and oxygen with a fusion reaction, 
which would explain why they do not appear as absorption lines in the spectrum of the supernova. Lastly, the companion stars 
were not observed near the explosion area (\cite{Li_2011}).
    \item \textbf{Double degenerate} (\cite{Iben_1984}): This occurs when a white dwarf star accretes its mass from a nearby less massive white dwarf star in close orbit.
    The combined mass of the system can exceed the stability limit of a white dwarf, leading to the fusion of carbon and a thermonuclear explosion that disrupts 
    the star. However, there are arguments against this scenario. Depending on the accretion rate, carbon ignition could be triggered earlier than anticipated which 
    in return can turn the white dwarf into a neutron star. 
    One argument is the possibility of the less massive white dwarf of forming an accretion disk around 
    its companion. 
    \item \textbf{Double degenerate} (\cite{Iben_1984}): This scenario occurs when two white dwarfs in a close binary 
    decaying due to gravitational wave emission. 
    During the merger, the less massive white dwarf can be disrupted and accreted by its companion. The resulting increase 
    in mass and density can trigger the ignition of carbon burning, leading to a thermonuclear explosion that disrupts the star. However, there are arguments 
    against this scenario. Depending on the merger dynamics and accretion rate, 
    an accretion-induced collapse can be achieved generating a neutron star rather than a thermonuclear explosion. Another concern is the possibility that the less massive 
    white dwarf forms an accretion disk around its companion.
\end{itemize}
Despite significant advances in our physical understanding of Type Ia 
supernovae, these models are not yet capable of delivering 
quantitative predictions at the precision required for cosmology. 
Cosmological analyses therefore rely on empirical models, calibrated 
on well-observed training samples.

\subsection{Explosion Rate}

The explosion rate of Type Ia supernovae is the measurement of the number of supernova explosions at a redshift $z$ in year per unit of comoving volume and restframe time. 
It can be divided into two categories: low redshift up to $z \sim 0.3$ and high redshift. At low redshift the explosion is nearly constant 
and barely evolves. Low redshift samples such as ZTF allow us to measure that rate. A study done by \cite{Perley_2020} on the ZTF sample, assuming an isotropic distribution, 
have measured a rate of;:
\begin{equation}
    R_{ZTF} = (2.35 \pm 0.24) \times 10^{4} \, \text{Gpc}^{-3}.\text{yr}^{-1}
\end{equation}
Where each supernova was weighted by the volume it probes with a varying limiting absolute magnitude. 
However, a recent paper by \cite{Lordet_2026} showed that the rate of supernova explosions might be more complex than anticipated and is much more dependent on its environment. 
Figure \ref{fig:sn_rate_antoine} highlights that the assumption that the rate is homogenous at low redshift isn't sufficient to describe supernova as we observe an excess 
at $z \sim 0.035$ while Figure \ref{fig:sn_vs_delta_antoine} shows that the excess of supernova at that redshift seems to be located around extremely dense regions. \\

At high redshift the rate becomes very dependent on the redshift. Its behaviour can be described by a power law with respect to $(1+z)$. Estimates by combining 
a multitude of probes in \cite{Frohmaier_2019} have shown that the rate can be described as:
\begin{equation}
    R(z) = (2.27 \pm 0.19)\times 10^{4} \times (1+z)^{(1.70\pm 0.21)} \, \text{Gpc}^{-3}.\text{yr}^{-1}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Observations} \label{sec:sn_observables}

\subsubsection{Spectroscopic}

Type Ia supernovae exhibit characteristic spectral features that evolve significantly with time. 
Most of the light emitted from a Type Ia supernova is in the visible restframe wavelengths with some parts of their spectra 
in the infrared and ultraviolet. The luminosity is powered by the radioactive decay of large quantities of 
nickel synthesized during the explosion following the decay chain:
$^{56}\text{Ni} \to \, ^{56}\text{Co} \to \, ^{56}\text{Fe} $. 

During the first weeks after the explosion, the ejecta remain optically thick and the observed spectrum originates near the photosphere. 
As the ejecta expand, the optical depth decreases and the photosphere progressively recedes through the ejecta, revealing deeper layers with different elements.
Around peak luminosity, the spectral features are strongly dominated by prominent Si II features at 4000$\AA$,  5800$\AA$ and 6150$\AA$ as 
well as other intermediate elements such as Ca II, Mg II and S II. 
In the weeks following maximum light emission, absorption from other elements such as Fe II, becomes increasingly prominent as the photosphere recedes into the inner ejecta.
Figure \ref{fig:sn_spectra_2011fe} 
shows the spectral sequence of SN 2011fe taken between -15 to 97 days after peak luminosity where we can see the notable spectral features of Type Ia supernovae. \\

\subsubsection{Photometric}

The photometric evolution of a Type Ia supernova is described by measuring its integrated flux through a set of observational filters, 
each covering a specific wavelength range. Historically, 
the standard filters used were U, B, V, R and I (also known as the Bessel filters \cite{Bessell_1990}) that cover the ultraviolet, optical and near-infrared range. 
Modern filters are generally denoted by $u$, $g$, $r$, $i$ and $z$ with additional filters in the infrared range such as $Y$. Figure \ref{fig:sn_lc_2009ig} shows the shape of the 
integrated fluxes in the Bessel filters. Each photometric curve is called a \textbf{light curve}. 
As we have mentioned previously, the luminosity of the supernova is primarily powered by the radioactive decay of the $^{56}\text{Ni}$ synthesized during the explosion. 
The luminosity increases for approximately 18 days 
reaching peak luminosity. 
Since the half-life of $^{56}\text{Ni}$ is only 6.1 days, a large fraction of the synthesized nickel has decayed by the time the supernova reaches maximum light. 
The peak luminosity is therefore closely related to the initial amount of $^{56}\text{Ni}$ produced during the explosion. The decay of 
$^{56}\text{Co}$, whose half-life is much longer (77 days), subsequently dominates the energy deposition and drives the late-time evolution of the light curve. 
At later times the curves experience quasi-exponential descent with a logarithmic slope that should match the decay expected from $^{56}\text{Co}$. 
As the supernova expands, the wavelength-dependent opacity of the medium varies. 
Consequently, the observed light curve shape in each photometric band is the result of the rate of energy deposition modulated by these opacity variations. 
This gives rise to features such as the secondary maximum observed in the near-infrared bands.
