\section{Cosmological Distances} \label{sec:cosmological_distances}

Observables such as the redshift or distances are used in order to constrain the matter content of the Universe as well as its expansion history and 
cosmological parameters. There are multiple ways one can define a distance in cosmology, each relying on a different set of parameters. \\

\subsection{Comoving Distances}

Under a FLRW metric, we can define the transverse comoving distance as the distance separating the observer and an event happening at $\chi$:
\begin{equation}
    d_p(\chi) = a_0 S_k(\chi)
\end{equation}
with $a_0$ being the scale factor at $t_0$. It is more practical to express $\chi$ as a function of the redshift since it is not a direct observable. If we consider a photon 
emitted at a time $t_e$ from the source object present at $\chi$ and received by the observer at $t_0$, then we get:
\begin{equation}
    \chi = c \int_{t_e}^{t_0} \frac{dt}{a(t)}
\end{equation}
By performing the following two variable changes:
\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{\dot{a}}{a} = \frac{1}{a} \frac{da}{dt} \Longleftrightarrow dt = \frac{da}{aH}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    1+z = \frac{a_0}{a} \Longleftrightarrow dz = -\frac{a_0}{a^2}da
\end{equation}

We get:
\begin{equation}
    \chi = \frac{c}{a_0} \int_{0}^{z} \frac{dz'}{H(z')}
\end{equation}
Which gives us the following equation for the physical distance:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{d_p(z) = a_0 S_k\left(\frac{c}{a_0} \int_{0}^{z} \frac{dz'}{H(z')}\right)}
\end{equation}
In a flat Universe, this expression is reduced to:
\begin{equation}
    d_p(z) = c \int_{0}^{z} \frac{dz'}{H(z')}
    \label{eqn:physical_distance}
\end{equation}

In practice, this distance isn't directly measurable as it requires to measure the values $H$ at every redshift along the line of sight. However, other observable distances exist 
that we can relate to the physical distance.\\

\subsection{Angular Distances}

We can first define the distance of an object based on its apparent size $D$ and angle $\Delta \theta$. We call this the angular distance which is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{d_A = \frac{D}{\delta \theta} = a(t)S_k(\chi) = \frac{1}{1+z} a_0 S_k\left(\frac{c}{a_0} \int_{0}^{z} \frac{dz'}{H(z')}\right)}
\end{equation}
Therefore knowing the redshift, the apparent angle and the size of an object gives us access to its distance.\\

\subsection{Luminosity Distances}

The luminosity distance $d_L$ relates the intrinsic luminosity of a 
source to the flux measured by a distant observer:
\begin{equation}
    f = \frac{L}{4\pi d_L^2}
    \label{eqn:flux_dl}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the observed flux and $L$ is the intrinsic luminosity of 
the source. To derive $d_L$ in an expanding universe, we consider a 
control surface: a sphere of comoving radius $\chi$ centred on the 
observer, over which the emitted photons are distributed. This 
surface has an area of $S = 4\pi a_0^2 S_k^2(\chi)$. 
One must account for the fact that the energy of each photon is reduced by a factor $(1+z)$ 
due to cosmological redshift, and the photon arrival rate is also 
reduced by a factor $(1+z)$ due to time dilation. 
This means that the observed luminosity will differ from the emitted luminosity giving us:
\begin{equation}
    L' = \frac{L}{(1+z)^2}
\end{equation}
where $L'$ is the luminosity seen by the observer. We can deduce that:
\begin{equation}
    f=\frac{L'}{S} = \frac{L}{(1+z)^2 4\pi a_0^2 S_k^2(\chi) }
\end{equation}
We can then identify a formula for the luminosity distance that is redshift dependent:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{d_L(z) = (1+z)a_0 S_k\left(\frac{c}{a_0} \int_{0}^{z} \frac{dz'}{H(z')}\right)}
    \label{eqn:distance_luminosity}
\end{equation}
We can see from the previous equations that:
\begin{equation}
    d_L(z) = d_A(z)(1+z)^2 = d_p(z)(1+z)
\end{equation}

At sufficiently low redshift all three distance expressions become equivalent and we can approximate it as:
\begin{equation}
    d_L(z) \approx \frac{cz}{H_0} \Leftrightarrow cz = v = H_0 d_L
    \label{eqn:dl_approx}
\end{equation}
where we can see that we recover the Hubble law. We compare in Figure \ref{fig:different_distances} the evolution of the three methods to measure distances $d_p$, $d_A$ and $d_L$.\\

\section{Cosmology with Type Ia Supernova Distances}
\subsection{Hubble Diagram}

The main tool used to constrain the expansion of the Universe with Type Ia supernovae is the Hubble diagram. For supernovae, the Hubble diagram maps their luminosity distances 
to their redshifts. In practice what we attempt to recover the supernova distances from their observed fluxes. The challenge is then to reconstruct the restframe fluxes 
of each supernova in one specific passband from observations. Hence, the requirement for measurements in multiple observer-frame passbands due to the redshifted fluxes. 
Conventionally, we attempt to reconstruct the restframe flux observed in the standard B band. 
Astronomical fluxes are historically reported in magnitudes, a 
logarithmic scale comparing the observed fluxes to a reference flux. Working 
in magnitudes is convenient because the distance information is 
encoded in a single quantity, the distance modulus $\mu$, which is 
directly related to the luminosity distance $d_L$ and therefore 
interchangeable with it throughout the cosmological analysis. We 
define these quantities as follows: 
The Type Ia supernova's magnitude in the standard B band denoted as $m_B$, is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    m_B = -2.5 \log_{10}\left( \frac{f}{f_0} \right)
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the integrated flux in the observer frame B band and $f_0$ a reference flux. We also define the absolute magnitude $M$ of an object with an intrinsic 
luminosity $L$ as the apparent magnitude if it was located at 10 pc from the observer. Given the definition of the luminosity distance we can get:
\begin{equation}
    M = -2.5 \log_{10}\left( \frac{L}{f_0 4 \pi (10 \, \text{pc})^2} \right)
\end{equation}
We can finally define the distance modulus of a Type Ia supernova as:
\begin{equation}
    \mu = m_B - M
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
    \mu
    &= -2.5 \log_{10}\left(\frac{f}{f_0}\right)
    + 2.5 \log_{10}\left(\frac{L}{4\pi (10\,\text{pc})^2 f_0}\right)\\
    &= 2.5 \log_{10}\left(
    \frac{L}{4\pi (10\,\text{pc})^2 f}
    \right)
\end{align}
and from Equation \ref{eqn:flux_dl} we can identify:

\begin{align}
    \mu
    &= 2.5 \log_{10}\left(
    \frac{L}{4\pi (10\,\text{pc})^2}
    \times
    \frac{4\pi d_L^2}{L}
    \right)\\
    &= 2.5 \log_{10}\left(
    \frac{d_L^2}{(10\,\text{pc})^2}
    \right)\\
\end{align}
Giving us:
\begin{equation}
    \mu = 5 \log_{10}\left( \frac{d_L}{10 \, \text{pc}} \right) = 5\log_{10}(d_L) + 25
    \label{eqn:dL_mu}
\end{equation}
where $d_L$ is the luminosity distance measured in Mpc. Modern Hubble diagrams are portrayed as $\mu$ against $z$. What is done in practice to construct a Hubble diagram is to 
recover restframe fluxes from the observed multi-band light curves using a spectrophotometric model. This must then be standardized to reduce the natural dispersion that exists between 
supernova before conducting any cosmological analysis. 

\subsubsection{Measurement of $\Omega_m$ and $w$}

Once we have access to the supernova Hubble diagram, we can constrain cosmological parameters. Under a flat $w$CDM cosmology, the luminosity 
distance (Equation \ref{eqn:distance_luminosity}) becomes:
\begin{equation}
    d_L = \frac{c(1+z)}{H_0} \int_{0}^{z} \frac{dz'}{E(z')}
    \label{eqn:dL_w}
\end{equation}
where $E(z) = H(z)/H_0$ is the dimensionless Hubble parameter. In a $w$CDM cosmology, the cosmological constant $\Lambda$ is replaced by a 
general dark energy component which we denote by $\Omega_{\text{DE}}$ with equation of state 
$w$ that can be different to -1. Neglecting the radiation contribution 
at the redshifts probed by supernovae ($z \lesssim 2$), and assuming a 
flat Universe, the dimensionless Hubble parameter reduces to:
\begin{equation}
    E(z) = \sqrt{\Omega_m (1+z)^3 + (1-\Omega_m)(1+z)^{3(1+w)}}
\end{equation}

By varying $\Omega_m$ and $w$ and integrating Equation \ref{eqn:dL_w}, 
one can compare the predicted distance moduli to the observed Hubble 
diagram and derive constraints on both parameters. The value of $H_0$ 
does not directly affect these constraints, as it is degenerate with 
the supernova absolute magnitude $M$ and is absorbed into a single 
nuisance parameter. We can see that in if we rewrite Equation \ref{eqn:dL_w} with $\mu$ instead we get:
\begin{align}
    \mu &= 
    5\log_{10}\left(\frac{c(1+z)}{H_0} \int_{0}^{z} \frac{dz'}{E(z')}\right) + 25 \\
    &= 5\log_{10}\left(c(1+z) \int_{0}^{z} \frac{dz'}{E(z')}\right) -5 \log_{10} (H_0) + 25 = m_B - M
\end{align}
Since $m_B$ is the observable quantity, the combination of the absolute magnitude and the Hubble constant can be absorbed into a single nuisance parameter 
$M' = M - 5 \log_{10} (H_0)$ which is treated as a constant when fitting cosmological parameters. 
The supernova Hubble diagram constrains a redshift-dependent combination 
of $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_{\text{DE}}$, resulting in a near-degeneracy 
between them. This degeneracy can be reduced by extending the supernova 
redshift range, requiring a low-$z$ sample at $z \lesssim 0.1$  and a high-$z$ sample at 
$0.1 \lesssim z \lesssim 2$. This degeneracy is further broken by combining supernovae with 
complementary probes such as BAO and the CMB.

\subsubsection{Measurement of $H_0$}

Measurements of $H_0$ with Type Ia supernova are done at low-$z$. It is possible to relate the magnitude of the supernova as a function of $H_0$ at low-$z$, however we 
will not be showing the demonstration here. The magnitude can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    m_B \approx A - 2.5\log_{10}(L H_0^2) + 5 \log_{10}(z)
\end{equation}
where $A$ is a constant. We can see that $H_0$ is degenerate with the 
intrinsic luminosity of the supernova $L$. Breaking this degeneracy requires us to calibrate the intrinsic supernova luminosity. 
We commonly use the "distance ladder" method that 
uses Cepheid stars in the Milky-Way as distance calibrators. \\

Cepheids are a class of stars that have variable periodic luminosities, which was first established by Henrietta Swan Leavitt thus establishing the Leavitt relation (\cite{Leavitt_1907}). Most recent 
observations of this relation were conducted by \cite{Breuval_2025}. Distances of Cepheids can be determined using "parallax": a method that measures the apparent displacement 
angle of an object with respect to a static background due to the motion of the observer. The application of this method is done by measuring the positions of the Cepheids with 
respect to a background of distant stars 6 months apart. The displacement angle of the observed Cepheid measured due to the half-rotation of the Earth around the Sun can be expressed 
as $\theta = \frac{d}{r}$ where $r$ is the distance to the Cepheid and $d$ the Earth-Sun distance. This means that the measurement of the distance of 
Cepheids can be calibrated using objects sufficiently close to the Milky Way for their distance to be measured by parallax.\\ 

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{MainLayout/Second_chapter/figures/leavitt.png}
    \caption{Original measurements by Leavitt establishing the magnitude period relation adjusted in \cite{Breuval_2025_b}.}
    \label{fig:cepheids}
\end{figure}

If we can identify nearby galaxies that contain both Cepheids and Type Ia supernovae, we can use the Leavitt relation to measure the Cepheid distance modulus 
$\mu_{\text{Cepheid}}$, which in turn calibrates the absolute magnitude $M$ of the supernova:
\begin{equation}
    M = m_B - \mu_{\text{Cepheid}}
\end{equation}
where $m_B$ is the observed peak magnitude of the Type Ia supernova in the rest-frame 
B band. This breaks the degeneracy between $L$ and $H_0$, as $M$ is now determined independently of the Hubble expansion. Applying this calibrated 
$M$ to Type Ia supernovae at low redshift, we can compute the luminosity distance $d_L$ from the observed magnitude and directly infer $H_0$ from Equation 
\ref{eqn:dl_approx}, averaged over a large sample of supernovae. The most recent measurement using this method yields $H_0 = 73.04 \pm 1.04$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$ 
(\cite{Riess_2022}).

\subsection{Peculiar Velocities} \label{sec:vp_methods}

We define the peculiar velocity of a celestial object as the velocity of an object relative to the homogeneous Hubble flow. This is particularly important for 
cosmological analyses as it can be used as a test for GR. If we assume peculiar velocities originate from the infall into gravitational potential wells then the detection 
of correlations between peculiar velocities directly probes the "strength" of gravity at cosmological scales. The parameter probed here is the growth rate of structures 
$f(z)\sigma_8(z)$ which combines the growth rate function $f$ as well as the matter fluctuations in a 8 Mpc box $\sigma_8$. A common parameterization of the growth rate 
of structures in $\Lambda$CDM is:
\begin{equation}
    f(z)\sigma_8(z) = \Omega_m(z)^{\gamma} \sigma_8(z)
\end{equation}
Where $\gamma$ is the growth index. For GR $\gamma$ is expected to be 0.55 therefore deviations from this value will indicate that GR is not the most suitable gravitational 
theory for the description of the large scale Universe. \\

\subsubsection{Extracting Peculiar Velocities}

Supernova peculiar velocity information is encoded in the observed redshift. If we define $z_{pv}$ as the redshift due to the transverse peculiar velocity of the supernova 
and $z_{cosmo}$ as the redshift due to the cosmic expansion, we get the following formula for the observed redshift $z_{obs}$ : 
\begin{equation}
    1+z_{obs} = (1+z_{cosmo})(1+z_{pv}) = (1+z_{cosmo})(1+\frac{v_{||}}{c})
\end{equation}
Where $v_{||}$ is the peculiar velocity. Measuring $z_{pv}$ requires knowledge of $z_{cosmo}$ which is not directly measurable. It can be deduced however once the expansion history 
has been determined. To further elaborate on that, if one knows exactly the expansion history, then one can directly deduce the cosmological redshift of an object based on how far away it is. This is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:vpec_carreres}. 
This entails that it is crucial to be able to accurately measure the distances of supernovae. Moreover, high redshift supernovae redshifts are almost entirely dominated by 
the cosmological redshift so analyses on supernova peculiar velocities will be done on low redshift samples. \\

\subsubsection{Measurement of $f\sigma_8$}

Multiple methods in inferring $f\sigma_8$ exist. We present here four different methods that exist. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Maximum likelihood estimation}} \cite{Gordon_2007, Carreres_2023}\\

The maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is a statistical method of estimating parameters given a model and a statistical distribution. The likelihood function measures how 
well the model explains the data by assimilating the statistical distribution. For MLE, the distribution is assumed to be Gaussian as the variable is the distance modulus of 
the supernova. The likelihood can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} \propto |\mathbf{C}(\theta)|^{-\frac{1}{2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{v}(\theta)^{T} \mathbf{C}(\theta)^{-1} \mathbf{v}(\theta) \right)
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ are the model parameters, $\mathbf{v}$ is the peculiar velocity calculated as the difference between the observed distance modulus and the predicted distance modulus defined by a model, 
$\mathbf{C}$ is the covariance matrix which can be divided into two parts $C = C_{\text{noise}} + C_{\text{PV}}$ where the second term directly depends on the value 
of $f\sigma_8$ and is left as a free parameter to be determined. The goal is to either maximize $\mathcal{L}$ or minimize its logarithmic value $\log \mathcal{L}$ 
which can be numerically beneficial. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Compressed two-point statistics}} \\

The compressed two-point statistics is done by adjusting the amplitude of the theoretical two-point velocity-velocity power spectrum to the data. If we would assume a $\Lambda$CDM cosmology, 
the power spectrum is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{vv}(k) \propto (aHf\sigma_8)^2 \int dk \mathcal{P}(k) j_1(kr)^2 \hat{\mathbf{r}}  \hat{\mathbf{r}}'
\end{equation}
where $j_1$ is the first order spherical Bessel function and $\mathcal{P}$ is the matter power spectrum. For a linear approximation we obtain:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{vv}(k) \propto (aHf\sigma_8)^2 \frac{\mathcal{P}(k)}{k^2}
\end{equation}
What remains is to then adjust the $f\sigma_8$ value to match the observed power spectrum. If the density field is indeed Gaussian, then the full power spectrum would 
contain all of the information on the distribution. However at small scales it is not the case. \\ 

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Density field reconstruction}} \\

The density field reconstruction infers the velocity field of matter from a galaxy density field. If we call $\delta_g$ the galaxy density field and $b$ the galaxy bias 
(term that models how well galaxies trace matter) then the reconstructed velocity field becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v} = \frac{aH \beta}{4\pi} \int d^3\mathbf{x'} \frac{\mathbf{x'} - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x'} - \mathbf{x}|^3} \delta_g(\mathbf{x'})
\end{equation}
where $\beta = \frac{f}{b}$. Comparing this to the velocity field inferred from Type Ia supernovae, one can determine $\beta$. If then this is combined with 
external measurements of $\sigma_{8,g} = b \sigma_8$, we can compute the growth rate of structures with $f\sigma_8 = \beta \sigma_{8,g}$. This has been done in 
\cite{Lavaux_2011}. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Field level forward modeling}} \\

The field level forward model treats the density field in itself as a free parameter. The idea is to evolve an initial simulated density field using a gravity model 
to obtain a final density and velocity field. The observables such as galaxies or supernovae are modeled within the inference to obtain the underlying density field. 
$$ \text{Initial field} \longrightarrow \text{Final field} \longrightarrow \text{Model observables} \Longleftrightarrow \text{Compare to observations} $$
Due to the nature of this method, the growth rate of structures can be directly determined via the final constrained simulation. The challenge of this method relies 
in multiple stages such as: determining an accurate gravity model while maintaining reasonable computational time and accurately modeling the relation between the observables 
and the density field. More details on field-level inferences is given in Chapter \ref{chap5}. \\
