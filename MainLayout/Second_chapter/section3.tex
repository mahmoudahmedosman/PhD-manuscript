\section{Distance Measurements from Observed Fluxes} \label{sec:dsitance_from_flux}

The key information in doing a cosmological analysis with Type Ia supernova is found in their rest-frame fluxes. The photometric fluxes as mentioned previously is a broadband 
flux, meaning it is integrated in an observation band. Due to the redshift of each supernova being different, these observer-frame wavelength region 
covered by a given filter will be different for an observer on Earth, 
which makes it difficult to compare the same physical quantities. This can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:flux_evolution}. The key is to be able to reconstruct the rest-frame flux in a given band 
from the observed fluxes measured in multiple bands. We use the rest-frame B band since it contains the maximum light emission from supernova. From now on we will call this band the 
$B^*$ band. Once we have obtained the flux in the $B^*$, we can obtain the distance modulus.\\

Despite their overall similarity, Type Ia supernova light curves display variations in their shape and luminosity. 
This variability can be largely accounted for using standardization 
parameters, hence Type Ia supernovae are standardizable candles. Without taking those effects into account, the dispersion that exists around peak magnitude in the standard B 
filter is around 0.4mag. The most well known standardization relation is the Tripp relation (\cite{Tripp_1999}) which uses two standardization parameters named stretch and 
color. After standardization, the dispersion can go down to 0.15mag. More complex standardization methods have been explored, however they necessitate spectral knowledge as well.\\

\section{Standardization}

\subsection{Brighter-slower Effect} 

The brighter-slower effect is an effect observed in Type Ia supernovae where the light curves tend to have a slower decline the higher the flux of the supernova (\cite{Phillips_1993}). We define the 
decline parameter $\Delta m_{15}$ as the difference of magnitude of the supernova in B band at the date of maximum emission and 15 days later:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta m_{15} =M_B(t=t_{max} + 15) -  M_B(t=t_{max})
\end{equation}

Historically, this relation was also described using the \textit{stretch} parameter $s$, which scales the temporal evolution of a light curve, i.e. its width. 
Modern empirical light curve models such as SALT2 (\cite{Guy_2007_SALT2}) instead introduce the parameter $X_1$, which corresponds to the amplitude of 
the first variability component of the spectrophotometric model (Section \ref{sec:SALT2}). Although $X_1$ is strongly correlated with the original stretch parameter, it is not itself a 
stretch factor. \\

This effect has been revised in \cite{Ginolin_2025_a} where this effect was measured on a sample of over 900 supernovae from the ZTF survey. It was demonstrated that this effect in fact 
presents two regimes that depend on the value of the stretch. In other words, the scaling of the brighter-slower effect varies based on the value of $X_1$. We refer 
to this variation as the "broken-alpha" effect. The results from \cite{Ginolin_2025_a} on the scale-dependent effect can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:broken_alpha}.\\

\subsection{Brighter-bluer Effect}

\subsection{Brighter-bluer Effect}

In a similar way, the brighter-bluer effect characterises how bluer supernovae tend to be have a higher flux (\cite{Hamuy_1995, 
Riess_1996}). We define an associated model parameter, the \textit{color} $c$, which is conventionally close to the difference between the B and V magnitudes 
at maximum light, but whose exact definition depends on the model and 
its training. When $c$ is negative the supernova is said to be blue, 
and when positive, redd. 
Several physical effects contribute to the observed color of a Type 
Ia supernova:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Host galaxy dust exitinction
    \item Intrinsic color variation for each supernova
    \item Eventual circumstellar dust
\end{enumerate}


This effect was confirmed in \cite{Ginolin_2025_b} where it was studied on the ZTF DR2 sample of over 900 supernovae. A scale-dependent effect was proposed in the paper but found no 
statistical significance for it. The results on the scale-dependent effect can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:broken_beta}.\\

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{MainLayout/Second_chapter/figures/broken_beta.png}
    \caption{Binned Hubble residuals as a function of color. The data is fitted to highlight the brighter-bluer effect (\cite{Ginolin_2025_b}).}
    \label{fig:broken_beta}
\end{figure}

The Tripp relation combines both effects to standardize the observed magnitude with two parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
    m_{Tripp} = m_{obs} + \alpha X_1 - \beta c
\end{equation}
where $m_{Tripp}$ is the standardized magnitude in the B band using the Tripp relation. \\

\subsection{Environmental Effects}

Studies conducted on large samples of Type Ia supernovae have shown that the standardized luminosity depends on properties of the host 
galaxy beyond the stretch and color corrections. 
This is seen as a \textit{step} in the Hubble residuals hen 
the supernova sample is split according to a given host galaxy 
property. 
One possible dependence would be the mass of the galaxies(\cite{Sullivan_2010}) the so-called \textit{mass step}. 
This dependence presents itself as an offset in the standardized when the population of host galaxies' masses are divided into two categories: more or less than $10^{10}M_{\odot}$. 
The mass step has shown a $3.9 \sigma$ significance after correcting for stretch and color (\cite{Sullivan_2010}). \\

Another dependence would be the star formation rate in those galaxies (\cite{Rigault_2020}). Galaxies can be divided into two subgroups: high star formation galaxies and 
low star formation galaxies. This step factor has shown a $2.6 \sigma$ significance after correcting for stretch and color. This dependence is seen in Figure \ref{fig:mass_step}. \\

The \cite{Roman_2018} paper studied a particularly significant dependence on the supernovae local $U-V$ rest-frame colors (limited to a certain radius around the supernova). 
This step factor has shown a $7.0 \sigma$ significance. When the same study was conducted on 927 ZTF supernovae with the local colors $g-z$, the significance was found to be 
around $18.2 \sigma$. \\

These environmental dependencies can be accounted for by adding an 
additional nuisance parameter $\gamma$ to the Tripp relation:
\begin{equation}
    m_{Tripp} = m_{obs} + \alpha X_1 - \beta c + \gamma \Delta_E
    \label{eqn:full_tripp}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_E$ is a step function of the chosen environmental 
observable, defined by a threshold value, and $\gamma$ is fitted 
alongside the other standardisation parameters. For the mass step 
specifically, $\Delta_E = \Delta_M$ takes a value that depends on 
whether the host galaxy mass exceeds the threshold of 
$10^{10} \, M_{\odot}$.
