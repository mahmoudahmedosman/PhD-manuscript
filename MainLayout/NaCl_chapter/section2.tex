\section{NaCl Framework}

The first part of my work for this thesis was the continuation of the development of a new framework designed to train spectrophotometric models of type Ia supernova called 
NaCl ("Nouveaux Algorithmes de Courbes de Lumière") which was initially started by G. Augarde during his PhD, defended in September of 2022 (\cite{augar2022}).

Following the exploratory work conducted in G. Augarde’s thesis, the 
framework required substantial refactoring to make it fully 
model-independent and capable of scaling to the approximately 3,000 
objects in the \textsc{Lemaître} dataset. I contributed to the redesign and 
implementation of the framework, performed the first tests on real 
data, and carried out the first extensive validation of the new 
framework using realistic simulated datasets.  

\subsection{Why a Spectrophotometric Model? }


NaCl is one of the main components of the cosmological inference 
pipeline described in the previous chapter. Its primary purpose is to 
infer from the supernova light-curves, measured in several 
observer-frame passbands, the restframe quantities required to 
determine standardized distances. \\

In the standardization scheme introduced by \cite{Tripp_1999} (Equation \ref{eqn:full_tripp}), 
these quantities are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $X_0$ or equivalently $m_B = -2.5 \log_{10}(X_0)$ which represent the SN restframe broadband flux, conventionally defined in the B-band
    \item $c$, a restframe color, conventionally defined in the B and V bands
    \item $X_1$ a restframe light curve shape parameter measuring the width of the light curve.
\end{enumerate}

None of these quantities are directly measurable from the 
observer-frame light curves. Instead, they must be inferred using a 
spectrophotometric model: $\mathcal{S}(p, \lambda; \theta)$, where $p$ is 
the rest-frame phase of the supernova and $\lambda$ the rest-frame 
wavelength and $\theta$ a set of parameters describing the diversity 
of supernovae: typically $X_1$ and $c$ in the Tripp scheme. \\

When combined with models of the telescope bandpasses, such a model 
predicts the observer frame supernova flux as a function of phase and 
the parameters $X_0, X_1, c$. These can then be estimated by fitting 
the predicted fluxes to the observed light curves through likelihood 
minimization. \\

The central challenge is therefore the determination of the model 
$\mathcal{S}(p,\lambda,\theta)$. The standard approach is to learn it directly 
from the data. The model may, for example, be decomposed on a spline 
basis, or implemented as a neural network, and its parameters fitted 
on a training sample. This sample typically includes light curves 
spanning a redshift range as broad as possible, as well as spectra 
that help capture the detailed spectral features of Type Ia supernovae 
and their diversity. \\

NaCl was developed specifically for the \textsc{Lemaître} cosmology inference 
pipeline. It comprises all the code needed to learn the model and 
determine the supernova parameters of interest.

\subsection{The Idea Behind the Framework}

Most existing models are separately trained on a specific dataset and 
distributed for use in other cosmological analyses. This procedure 
carries the uncertainties associated with the training sample into the 
downstream analysis, including those arising from the finite size of 
the training sample and from its calibration uncertainties. \\

One of the specificities of NaCl is to merge training and light curve 
fit. This makes it possible to propagate training and calibration 
uncertainties consistently throughout the analysis, while ensuring 
that the learned model captures the specific diversity of the 
cosmological sample under study. \\

NaCl has been designed to be model-agnostic. The model is cleanly 
separated from the likelihood and minimization code, which makes it 
easy to develop and test alternative parameterizations of the 
supernova diversity. As of today, the main NaCl model follows the 
SALT2/SALT3 parametrization, of the form: $$ \mathcal{S}(\lambda,p) = [M_0(\lambda,p) + X_1M_1(\lambda, p)]10^{0.4cCL(\lambda)} $$. 
In this model, the diversity 
of Type Ia supernovae is therefore described by two parameters, $X_1$ 
and $c$, which determine how the mean spectrophotometric sequence is 
modified for a given supernova. \\

More generally, NaCl provides the inference machinery required to map 
the information contained in the photometric and spectroscopic 
observations onto the diversity space defined by a given supernova 
model. For the SALT2/SALT3 parameterization, this amounts to 
describing each supernova through the two parameters $X_1$ and $c$. 
The same machinery can, however, use alternative models in which the 
supernova diversity is described by a larger number of parameters, or 
by more general, potentially non-linear latent spaces. 

\subsection{Differences with the SALT Models}


The NaCl implementation of the SALT2 model differs from the original 
SALT2 training procedure in several respects. First, SALT2 relies on 
an iterative procedure, alternating between the determination of the 
date of maximum light for each supernova, $t_{max}$, and the estimation of the 
spectrophotometric model. In NaCl, by contrast, the date of maximum 
light of each supernova is included directly in the parameter vector 
and optimized simultaneously with the model parameters. \\

Second, the SALT2 training procedure, alternates between the 
determination of the spectrophotometric model and that of the error 
snake, which accounts for the remaining SN-to-SN variability not 
captured by the model. In NaCl, the model and error model are inferred 
jointly in a single simultaneous fit. \\

Finally, NaCl removes the formal distinction between model training 
and light curve fitting. This unified formulation facilitates the 
propagation of uncertainties associated with the finite size of the 
sample, measurement noise, and calibration systematics throughout the 
analysis. At the end of the fit, NaCl provides a covariance matrix 
that consistently accounts for these different sources of uncertainty. 
Marginalizing over the model-specific parameters then yields the 
covariance matrix of the parameters of interest. \\

A central challenge is to ensure that this covariance matrix correctly 
captures the variability that would arise from one training sample to
another. This is validated using ensembles of mock datasets, as 
described in the final section of this chapter. \\

\subsection{Practical Implementation of the SALT2 Model}

\subsubsection{Model}

In order to determine the global model surfaces $M_0$ and $M_1$, 
we describe them using two-dimensional cubic B-splines. B-splines are piecewise polynomial 
basis functions defined over a set of knots, which can be irregularly spaced, and provide a 
flexible way of modeling smooth empirical functions and surfaces. In NaCl, the $M_0$ and $M_1$ surfaces are defined on a 
two-dimensional grid in rest-frame phase $p$ and wavelength $\lambda$ as products of one-dimensional B-spline 
bases along each axis, with the corresponding coefficients fitted directly from the training data:
\begin{equation}
    M_{0|1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_p} \sum_{l=1}^{n_{\lambda}} \theta_{kl}^{[0|1]} \mathcal{B}_k(p) \mathcal{B}_l(\lambda)
    \label{eqn:model_spline}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{B}$ are the B-spline basis functions and 
$\theta_{kl}^{[0|1]}$ are the model parameters inferred simultaneously 
with the light curve parameters of each supernova during training. This 
structure implies that the spectral shape at any given 
phase is a linear combination of the same wavelength basis functions, 
with coefficients that vary smoothly with phase. The total number of 
free parameters per surface is $n_p \times n_\lambda$. We use $n_p=30$ between phases of -50 and 100 days and 
$n_\lambda = 127$ for wavelengths between $2000\, \AA$ and $9000\, \AA$, amounting to 3810 parameters for each surface with a knot 
every 5 days and $55 \, \AA$.

Similarly, the filter transmission curves 
are evaluated on the same spline basis as the model, ensuring 
consistency between the model and the photometric predictions, they are expressed as $T(\lambda) = \sum_q t_q \mathcal{B}_q(\lambda)$. The 
color law $CL(\lambda)$ is implemented as a degree 5 polynomial, 
sufficient for its simpler one-dimensional structure. It introduces 4 free parameters to be determined alongside the rest of the model parameters.  \\

\subsubsection{Predicting Broadband Fluxes}

The broadband flux in a transmission band $X$ is expressed as (see Equation \ref{eqn:boradband_flux}):
\begin{equation}
    f_X((1+z)p) = X_0 (1+z) [I_0(p) + X_1I_1(p)]
\end{equation}
with:
\begin{equation}
    I_{0|1} = \int M_{0|1}(\lambda, p) \frac{\lambda}{hc}T_X((1+z)\lambda) 10^{0.4cCL(\lambda)} \text{d}\lambda
\end{equation}
By decomposing the model components on the B-spline basis we obtain:
\begin{equation}
    I_{0|1} = \sum_{klq} \theta_{kl,0|1} t_q[z] \mathcal{B}_k(p) \int \mathcal{B}_l(\lambda) \frac{\lambda}{hc} \mathcal{B}_q((1+z)\lambda) 10^{0.4cCL(\lambda)} \text{d}\lambda
\end{equation}
The color law has a slower variation compared to the other functions in the integral. Therefore to simplify and accelerate the calculation, we consider it as a constant in the 
integration space yielding:
\begin{equation}
    I_{0|1} \approx \sum_{klq} \theta_{kl,0|1} t_q[z] \mathcal{B}_k(p) \int \mathcal{B}_l(\lambda) \frac{\lambda}{hc} \mathcal{B}_q((1+z)\lambda)\text{d}\lambda \times 10^{0.4cCL(\bar{\lambda})} 
\end{equation}
with:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\lambda} \equiv \lambda_{lq}^{\text{eff}} \equiv \frac{
        \int \lambda^2 \mathcal{B}_l(\lambda) \mathcal{B}_q((1+z)\lambda) \text{d}\lambda
    }{
        \int \lambda \mathcal{B}_l(\lambda) \mathcal{B}_q((1+z)\lambda) \text{d}\lambda
    }
\end{equation}
The value $\bar{\lambda}$ is judiciously chosen as an effective wavelength, 
weighted by the contribution of the basis functions 
$\mathcal{B}_l(\lambda)$ and $\mathcal{B}_q((1+z)\lambda)$ to the integral. 
The additional factor of $\lambda$ in this weighting directly arises 
from the factor $\lambda$ present in the integrand. 

The remaining integral only involves the B-spline basis functions, which are piecewise polynomials and can therefore be evaluated efficiently. 
Moreover, these integrals define the elements of the Gramian matrix $G_{lq}[z]$. The expression can then be written as:
\begin{equation}
    I_{0|1} \approx \sum_{klq} \theta_{kl,0|1} t_q[z] \mathcal{B}_k(p) G_{lq}[z] \times 10^{0.4cCL(\bar{\lambda})} 
\end{equation}
$G_{lq}[z]$ is sparse and can be pre-calculated, enabling efficient evaluation of the integrals required to predict the broadband fluxes. 

\subsubsection{Predicting Spectra} 


In practice, the spectrophotometric model is constrained jointly by 
light-curve and spectral data. Spectra are particularly important for 
recovering the fine-scale spectral structure of Type Ia supernovae. 
Their flux calibration, however, is generally much less accurate than 
that of photometric light curves. While the latter can be calibrated 
to sub-percent precision, long-slit spectra may suffer from 
wavelength-dependent calibration errors at the level of 1 to 10\%, 
depending on the instrument and reduction procedure. 

It is therefore important to prevent these calibration errors from 
biasing the broadband spectral shape of the trained model. During 
training, the smooth wavelength-dependent component of each spectrum 
is absorbed by a spectrum-specific nuisance polynomial, denoted $SpecRecal(\lambda)$, so that the 
spectrum primarily constrains the small-scale spectral features. One 
polynomial is introduced for each spectrum and fitted simultaneously 
with the other model parameters. 

The predicted spectrum for a supernova is then expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\text{spec}}((1+z)\lambda, (1+z)p) = \frac{SpecRecal(\lambda)}{1+z} [M_0(\lambda,p) + X_1M_1(\lambda, p)]
\end{equation}


\subsubsection{Constraints}

Because the model is fully empirical and highly flexible, some 
transformations of the model surfaces can be compensated by 
corresponding transformations of the individual supernova parameters. 
This gives rise to degeneracies between the global model parameters 
and the supernova parameters of interest. A set of constraints must 
therefore be imposed during training in order to break these 
degeneracies and define a unique parameterization of the model. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Model normalization:}} \\

There is a degeneracy 
between the surface parameters and the global values of the $X_0$ 
parameters. We therefore need to fix the model normalization at 
maximum luminosity. This is done with 4 constraints on the $M_0$ and $M_1$ 
surfaces:

\begin{align}
    \int M_0(\lambda,p=0)\frac{\lambda}{hc}B(\lambda) d\lambda = 1 \label{eqn:M0norm}\\
    \int M_1(\lambda,p=0)\frac{\lambda}{hc}B(\lambda) d\lambda = 0
\end{align}
where $B(\lambda)$ is the restframe standard $B$-band.\\


\noindent \textbf{\underline{Date of maximum emission:}} \\

There is a degeneracy between the spectrophotometric model and the date of maximum emission $t_{\max}$, since a shift in $t_{\max}$ can be compensated by a 
corresponding shift of the model along the phase axis. To fix this degeneracy, we impose that the model reaches its maximum in the standard B-band at $p=0$. 
This condition is applied to $M_0$ and $M_1$:

\begin{align}
    \frac{d}{dp} \int M_0(\lambda,p=0)\frac{\lambda}{hc}B(\lambda)d\lambda \Big{|}_{p=0} = 0 \\
    \frac{d}{dp} \int M_1(\lambda,p=0)\frac{\lambda}{hc}B(\lambda)d\lambda \Big{|}_{p=0} = 0
\end{align}
\\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Color Law:}} \

A degeneracy also exists between the spectral shape of the model and the color law $CL$. A smooth tilt of the spectral components $M_0$ and $M_1$ can be 
compensated by a corresponding change in $CL$. To fix this degeneracy, we impose:
\begin{equation}
CL(\lambda^B) = 0, \qquad CL(\lambda^V) = 1,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda^B$ and $\lambda^V$ denote the central wavelengths of the rest-frame standard B and V bands, respectively. \\


\noindent \textbf{\underline{Average color:}} \\

Another degeneracy exists between the average spectral color of $M_0$ and the individual supernova color parameter $c$. A change in the overall color of 
$M_0$ can be compensated by a corresponding shift in $c$. To fix this degeneracy, we define the average supernova color such that $c$ describes the relative 
color variation of individual supernovae with respect to this reference. We impose that the $M_0$ surface has $B-V=0$ at peak luminosity. 
The corresponding constraint is:
\begin{equation}
\int M_0(\lambda,p=0) \left[B(\lambda)-V(\lambda)\right] \frac{\lambda}{hc} d\lambda=0
\end{equation}
\\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Average stretch:}} \\

A similar degeneracy also exists with $X_1$. A global change of $X_1$ can be absorbed by changing the shape of the $M_0$ surface along the phase axis. 
We therefore need to define the average stretch of the model. 
We define the average stretch such that the effect of $X_1$ on the model is consistent with the SALT2 stretch convention. 
To enforce this correspondence, we require the difference between the model evaluated at $X_1=0$ and $X_1=1$ at a 
phase of $p=15$ days to match the corresponding flux difference measured in SALT2:

\begin{equation}
    \int \left[M_0(\lambda, p=15) + M_1(\lambda, p=15) \right] \frac{\lambda}{hc}B(\lambda) d\lambda - \alpha \int M_0(\lambda, p=15) \frac{\lambda}{hc}B(\lambda) d\lambda = 0
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 10^{-0.4 \times 0.162}$ the flux difference found in SALT2. \\

\noindent \textbf{\underline{Average width:}} \\

The final remaining degree of freedom concerns the overall width of the model. While the definition of $X_1$ specifies the relative variation of the model with stretch, 
it does not fix its overall scale. The model can therefore be rescaled without affecting the constraints imposed on the other parameters. We remove this freedom by 
imposing a constraint at a fixed phase. We choose $p=15$ days, corresponding to $\Delta m_{15}$, and we impose:

\begin{equation}
    \int M_0(\lambda, p=15) \frac{\lambda}{hc} d\lambda = 10^{-0.4}
\end{equation} \\


\subsubsection{Error Models}

Although the SALT2-like parametrization implemented in NaCl describes 
the diversity of Type Ia supernova quite well, it does not fully capture the 
supernova-to-supernova variability. Future models should be able to provide a better 
description of the supernova diversity, as suggested in the exploratory work 
of \cite{Stein_2022_PAE} for example. The remaining supernova-to-supernova diversity not 
captured by the model is accounted for in an error snake, i.e. an 
error model of the form:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{V}_{\text{error}} = \sigma_{\text{SN}} \times \mathbf{M}(\lambda,p)
    \label{eqn:error_model}
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the model evaluation and $\sigma_{\text{SN}}$ is the error model parameter which is fitted along 
with the main model. 

As we will see below, fitting an error model complicates significantly 
the log-likelihood, since the covariance matrix is no longer constant 
but varies as a function of a subset of the fit parameters. An illustration 
on the impact of the error model can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:errorsnake}.

\subsubsection{Regularization}

Since the training dataset contains a finite number of supernovae, not 
all regions of the phase-wavelength space are equally well sampled. In 
particular, the model surfaces $M_0$ and $M_1$ are poorly constrained 
at phases far from maximum light, where fewer observations are available, 
and near the boundaries of the phase range where data points may fall 
outside the model support. Without additional constraints, the 
optimization would produce large unphysical variations of the spline 
parameters $\theta_{kl}$ in these regions. Regularization addresses 
this by adding a penalty term that suppresses such 
variations, effectively encoding the prior expectation that the model 
surfaces should be smooth and close to zero in unconstrained regions.

The regularization penalty takes the form:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\text{reg}} \sum_{kl} \left( \theta_{k,l}^2 + 
    \left( \theta_{k+1,l} - \theta_{k,l} \right)^2 \right)
    \label{eqn:old_reg}
\end{equation}
where the first term penalises large values of the model parameters, 
pulling them toward zero in regions with no constraining data, and the 
second term penalises large differences between neighbouring parameters 
along the phase axis, suppressing sharp variations in the model. The 
hyperparameter $\mu_{\text{reg}}$ controls the overall strength of the 
regularization: a large value enforces strong smoothness at the cost 
of model flexibility, while a small value allows the model to follow 
the data more closely at the risk of overfitting. 
In matrix form this 
penalty can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\text{reg}} \boldsymbol{\theta}^T 
    \mathbf{P} \boldsymbol{\theta}
    \label{eqn:chi2_reg_old}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{P}$ is the regularization matrix encoding both penalty 
terms in Equation \ref{eqn:old_reg} and $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is the 
vector of all model parameters.
Practically, this penalty is accounted for in the likelihood (Section \ref{sec:nacl_likelihood}).

This regularization, however, introduces a potential dependence on the 
hyperparameter $\mu_{\text{reg}}$. If its value is too large, the 
regularization can overly constrain the model and potentially bias the 
recovered cosmological parameters. Conversely, if it is too small, the 
model can develop strong variations in regions that are poorly 
constrained by the data. An illustration of this effect is shown in 
Figure \ref{fig:regularization}.

As part of this work, we explored alternative forms of regularization 
that could reduce this dependence on a fixed regularization strength. 
An adaptive regularization scheme, inspired by 
\cite{lenz2022adaptiveregularizationbsplinemodels}, was developed for 
NaCl in which the local strength of the regularization is adjusted 
according to the amount of data constraining each region of the model. 
However, when applied during the determination of the best-fit parameters, 
this approach introduced convergence issues and prevented the 
minimizer from reaching a converged solution. It is therefore 
not included in the current version of NaCl and would require further 
development beyond the scope of this PhD. 

\subsubsection{Propagation of the Calibration Uncertainties}

The calibration uncertainties are incorporated directly into the model through a set of nuisance parameters. 
One parameter $\eta_X$ is introduced for each transmission band in the dataset, with a prior determined from 
the corresponding calibration uncertainty. For the \textsc{Lemaître} project, we consider 12 transmission bands and 
therefore introduce 12 calibration parameters. The predicted photometric flux in band $X$ becomes:

\begin{equation}
\phi_X((1+z)p) = (1+\eta_X) f_X((1+z)p),
\end{equation}

where $f_X$ is the broadband flux predicted by the spectrophotometric model.


\subsubsection{Log-likelihood} \label{sec:nacl_likelihood}

Assembling all the constituants developed in the last sections,
we are able to build the NaCl likelihood which indicates the probability 
of observing a dataset $\mathbf{Y}$, comprised of $N$ photometric and spectroscopic measurements, given a set of parameters $\boldsymbol{\beta}$.
We define the model residuals as $\mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{Y} - \mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ where $\mathbf{M}$ is the model evaluation. 
We assume that the measurement uncertainties are Gaussian, meaning if our model $\mathbf{M}$ exactly predicts the measurements given a set of 
parameters $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{true}}$ then:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{true}}) + \mathbf{n}
\end{equation}
with 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{n} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{V})
\end{equation}
where $ \mathbf{V}$ is the covariance matrix. Therefore, the probability of observing the data given the model parameters is Gaussian. 
The likelihood then becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{\left(2 \pi \right)^{N/2} |\mathbf{V}|^{1/2}} \exp \left[
        -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}^{T} \mathbf{V}^{-1} \mathbf{R}
    \right]
\end{equation}
By choosing the set of parameters that best describes the data, the quantity becomes maximal. Numerically, it is more stable to deal with the with the negative 
log-likelihood written as:
\begin{equation}
    -2 \ln (\mathcal{L}) = N\ln(2\pi) + \ln(|\mathbf{V}|) + \mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{R}
\end{equation}
With $\mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{R}$ is also known as the $\chi^2$. The goal is to then minimize this quantity. \\

%The model constraints are enforced within the log-likelihood as a penalty. We define $\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ as the vector describing the distance from 
%each constraint to its expected value. The penalty on the log-likelihood is written as:
%\begin{equation}
%    \chi^2_{\text{cons}} = \mu_{\text{cons}} \mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta})^T \mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
%\end{equation}
where $\mu_{\text{cons}}$ is a hyperparameter that is adjusted beforehand. 

The model regularization is enforced within the log-likelihood as a penalty. The expression of this penalty is identical to Equation \ref{eqn:chi2_reg_old}. 
We denote this penalty as $\chi^2_{\text{reg}}$. 

Accounting for the error model in the log-likelihood is done at the level of the covariance matrix. We denote $V_{\text{meas}}$ as the covariance matrix from the measurements 
and $\mathbf{V}_{\text{error}}$ the covariance matrix from the error model as express in Equation \label{eqn:error_model}, then the full covariance matrix becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{V}^{-1} = \left( \mathbf{V}_{\text{meas}} + \mathbf{V}_{\text{error}} \right)^{-1}
\end{equation}
We see that this implementation complicates the problem of minimizing the negative log-likelihood as the covariance matrix is now dependent on the $\gamma$ parameter which is 
fitted during the minimization. 

Lastly, we must account for the propagation of calibration uncertainties. 
The calibration parameters are constrained by prior knowledge of the calibration uncertainties. 
The expected amplitude of these calibration variations, as well as their correlations between transmission bands, 
is determined from the calibration uncertainties. We model these uncertainties with a multivariate Gaussian prior centred on zero, 
with covariance matrix $V_\eta$:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\eta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},V_\eta)
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ is the vector containing the calibration parameters for all transmission bands. 
The covariance matrix $V_\eta$ therefore encodes both the uncertainty associated with each calibration parameter and the correlations between 
parameters corresponding to different transmission bands. This results in a penalty contribution to the log-likelihood: 
\begin{equation}
\chi^2_{\text{calib}} =
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{T}V_\eta^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}
\end{equation}

Regrouping all of these terms together, we obtain the full NaCl log-likelihood denoted as $Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})$:
%\begin{multline}

%    = N\ln(2\pi) + \ln(|\left( \mathbf{V}_{\text{meas}} + \mathbf{V}_{\text{error}} \right)|) + \mathbf{R}^T\left( \mathbf{V}_{\text{meas}} + \mathbf{V}_{\text{error}} \right)^{-1}\mathbf{R} \\
%    + \mu_{\text{cons}} \mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta})^T \mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) + \mu_{\text{reg}} \boldsymbol{\theta}^T \mathbf{P} \boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\eta}^{T}V_\eta^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}
%\end{multline}
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
    Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})
    ={}& N\ln(2\pi) + \ln(|\mathbf{V}|)
    + \mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{R}
    + \chi^2_{\text{cons}}
    + \chi^2_{\text{reg}}
    + \chi^2_{\text{calib}} \\[0.5em]
    ={}& N\ln(2\pi)
    + \ln\left(
    \left|\mathbf{V}_{\text{meas}}+\mathbf{V}_{\text{error}}\right|
    \right) \\
    &+ \mathbf{R}^T
    \left(\mathbf{V}_{\text{meas}}+\mathbf{V}_{\text{error}}\right)^{-1}
    \mathbf{R} \\

    + \mu_{\text{reg}}\boldsymbol{\theta}^T
    \mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\theta}
    + \boldsymbol{\eta}^T V_\eta^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}.
    \end{aligned}
    }
    \end{equation}

\subsubsection{Minimizer}
 
The general idea of minimizing the log-likelihood, $Q(\boldsymbol{\beta})$, is to start from a set of parameters $\boldsymbol{\beta}_i$ at an instance $i$ and determine the 
step of the parameters $\delta \boldsymbol{\beta}$ that approaches to the optimal set of parameters that describes the data. 
The procedure goes as follows. At each iteration, the $Q$ is approximated at its second order Taylor expansion:
\begin{equation}
    Q( \boldsymbol{\beta}_i + \delta \boldsymbol{\beta} ) \approx 
    Q( \boldsymbol{\beta}_i) + \mathbf{g}_i^T \delta \boldsymbol{\beta} + \frac{1}{2} \delta \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \mathbf{H}_i \delta \boldsymbol{\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{g}_i$ is the gradient of the log-likelihood evaluated at $\boldsymbol{\beta}_i$
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{g}_i = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_i} Q
\end{equation}
and $\mathbf{H}$ is the Hessian matrix evaluated at $\boldsymbol{\beta}_i$
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{H}_i = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_i}^2 Q
\end{equation}
The Newton-Raphson method is then employed to find the minimum value of the approximation by setting its derivative to zero:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial Q}{\partial \delta \boldsymbol{\beta}} \approx \mathbf{g}_i + \mathbf{H}_i \delta \boldsymbol{\beta} = 0
\end{equation}
This gives the parameter update step:
\begin{equation}
    \delta \boldsymbol{\beta} = - \mathbf{H}_i^{-1} \mathbf{g}_i
\end{equation}
However, the inversion of the Hessian can be problematic if the matrix is not positive-definite. To overcome that, a Levenberg-Marquardt scheme is applied before 
solving the equation. This consists in augmenting the diagonal of the Hessian as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \mathbf{H} + \lambda \mathbf{D}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is a damping parameter and $\mathbf{D}$ is a damping matrix that determines the charge of the diagonal of the Hessian. There are four different ways to modify
the diagonal of the Hessian:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{D} = 
    \begin{cases}
        \mathbf{I} \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \text{Levenberg:} \quad \qquad \tilde{H}_{ii} = H_{ii} + \lambda \\
        \text{diag}(\mathbf{H} ) \qquad \qquad \quad \text{Marquardt:} \qquad \quad \tilde{H}_{ii} = (1+ \lambda) H_{ii} \\
        \max_j (H_{jj}) \mathbf{I} \qquad \quad \text{Marquardt-max:} \quad \tilde{H}_{ii} = H_{ii} + \lambda \max_j (H_{jj}) \\
        \max_{\text{history}} (H_{jj}) \mathbf{I} \quad \text{Marquardt-lmax:} \quad \tilde{H}_{ii} = H_{ii} + \lambda \max_{\text{history}} (H_{ii}) 
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The equation to solve then becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \delta \boldsymbol{\beta} = - \tilde{\mathbf{H}}_i^{-1} \mathbf{g}_i
\end{equation}
In practice, inverting the entire matrix at every step would be computationally taxing. Instead, we exploit the sparsity of the Hessian 
and employ a Cholesky decomposition: $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T$ where $\mathbf{L}$ is a lower triangular matrix. 
This greatly reduces the computational cost of solving the linear 
system $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\delta\boldsymbol{\beta} = -\mathbf{g}$ 
compared to direct matrix inversion.

Lastly, to enforce that the model constraints are respected, 
the optimization of the parameters becomes a constrained minimization via Lagrange multipliers. We denote the linearized constraints as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{c}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}$ is the constraint matrix and $\mathbf{c}$ are the constraint to be respected by each parameter in $\boldsymbol{\beta}$. In the case of our minimization this becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_i + \delta \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{c}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}\delta \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}_i
\end{equation}
we denote $\mathbf{r}_{c,i} = \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}_i$ the constraint residuals

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}\delta \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{r}_{c,i}
\end{equation}
This is a second requirement needed to satisfied by $\delta \boldsymbol{\beta}$ in order to obtain the next step. 
This necessitates the introduction of additional Lagrange multipliers. We will omit the full demonstration of how the multipliers are injected in this thesis and only show the 
final results on the step:
\begin{equation}
    \delta \boldsymbol{\beta} = - \tilde{\mathbf{H}}_i^{-1} (\mathbf{g}_i + \mathbf{A}^T \boldsymbol{\mu}_{L,i})
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{L}$ are the Lagrange multipliers that are calculated with:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_{L,i} = \left( \mathbf{A} \tilde{\mathbf{H}}_i^{-1} \mathbf{A}^T\right)^{-1} \left[ \mathbf{r}_{c,i} - \mathbf{A} \tilde{\mathbf{H}}_i^{-1} \mathbf{g}_i \right]
\end{equation}

Once the step  $\delta \boldsymbol{\beta}$ has been computed, we calculate the differences of the log-likelihood, $\delta Q$, with the new set of parameters. If the negative log-likelihood 
is decreasing, the step is accepted. If not, the damping parameter $\lambda$ is updated at a larger value and the step is recalculated. The minimization stops once 
$|\delta Q|<\delta_{\text{stop}}$ where $\delta_{\text{stop}}$ is chosen to be small enough that the log-likelihood is near its minimum. 


\subsubsection{Covariance Matrix}

Due to the presence of constraints, the derivation of the fit 
covariance matrix presents a small subtlety that needs to be 
documented here. The $n_{id}$ independent constraints restrict the 
parameter space to an affine subspace of dimension $n-n_{id}$, $n$ being the total number of measurements, and 
therefore suppress fluctuations along $n_{id}$ independent directions. As a 
consequence, fluctuations of the fit parameters are restricted to the 
directions compatible with the constraints, and the covariance matrix 
inferred directly from the Fisher matrix must be modified accordingly. 

In the present implementation, the unconstrained Fisher matrix 
contains exact or nearly exact degeneracies associated with the model 
parametrization. A very small regularization is applied in practice in 
order to make its inversion numerically well defined. In the 
following, $\mathbf{W}$ denotes the inverse of this slightly regularized Fisher 
matrix. 


Formally, the corresponding local Gaussian approximation can be 
written as 
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_0, \mathbf{W})
\end{equation}
while the $n_{id}$ linear 
constraints are expressed in matrix form as: 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{c}
\end{equation}
Imposing these constraints amount to conditioning the Gaussian 
distribution on $\mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\beta} =\mathbf{c}$. The resulting distribution is again 
Gaussian: 
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathbf{c} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{W}_{\text{cond}})
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the solution of the constrained problem. 


Let us first recall the general expression for the conditional 
covariance of a multivariate Gaussian. If $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are jointly 
Gaussian: 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{x} \\ \mathbf{y} \end{pmatrix}
    \sim \mathcal{N}(
        \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_x\\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_y\end{pmatrix}, 
        \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{W}_{xx} & 
            \mathbf{W}_{xy} \\ \mathbf{W}_{yx} & \mathbf{W}_{yy}\end{pmatrix})
\end{equation}
then the distribution of $\mathbf{x}$ 
conditional on $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{c}$ is also Gaussian (e.g. \cite{Bishop_2006}, Chapter 2.3.1), 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{c} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{cond}},\mathbf{W}_{\text{cond}})
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{cond}} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_x + \mathbf{W}_{xy}\mathbf{W}_{yy}^{-1}(\mathbf{c}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_y)
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W}_{\text{cond}} = \mathbf{W}_{xx} - \mathbf{W}_{xy} \mathbf{W}_{yy}^{-1} \mathbf{W}_{yx}
\end{equation}

For our problem, we identify 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}= \boldsymbol{\beta}, \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\beta}
\end{equation}
so that 
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_x = \boldsymbol{\mu}_0, \qquad \boldsymbol{\mu}_y = \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\mu}_0
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W}_{xx} = \mathbf{W} \qquad \mathbf{W}_{xy} = \mathbf{W} \mathbf{A}^T \qquad \mathbf{W}_{yy} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{W} \mathbf{A}^T, \boldsymbol{\mu}_y = \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\mu}_0
\end{equation}


The mean of the conditional distribution is thus 
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_0 + \mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}^T(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}^T)^{-1}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}_0)
\end{equation}
showing that the constraints generally 
shift the solution with respect to the center of the unconstrained 
Gaussian approximation. In practice, this expression does not need to 
be evaluated, since $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is obtained directly from the constrained 
minimization. 

The covariance matrix under constraints is therefore: 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W}_{\text{cond}} = \mathbf{W} - \mathbf{W} \mathbf{A}^T (\mathbf{A} \mathbf{W} \mathbf{A}^T)^{-1} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{W} 
\end{equation}
Unlike the conditional mean, this 
covariance does not depend on $\mathbf{c}$: it only depends on the orientation 
of the constraint subspace, through $\mathbf{A}$, and on the local curvature of 
the likelihood, through $\mathbf{W}$. 

This is the covariance matrix associated with the fit parameters once 
the linear constraints are imposed.
