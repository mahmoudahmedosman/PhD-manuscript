In the previous chapter, we have presented the motivations and potential outcomes of conducting a field-level inference as well as the BORG algorithm. 
BORG has proven to be very successful 
when applied to galaxy catalogues and has shown great potential regarding weak lensing. In this PhD work, the goal is

\section{BORG-Peculiar Velocity}

The BORG-Peculiar Velocity (BORG-PV) algorithm is the same as the traditional BORG algorithm with the main difference being in the tracer modeling and likelihood. For BORG-PV, we 
will be using the supernovae distance moduli along with their redshifts as input. The goal will be to then infer the underlying density and velocity fields. 
There are two major differences with galaxy surveys: the first is at the level of the likelihood and the second is in the bias model that connects the matter density field with 
the observables. 
For the case of supernovae we will be relying on a simple phenomonological approach which will be described in the 
following section alongside the supernova modeling and likelihood. \\ 

\subsection{Initial likelihood}

In this section we describe the initial version of the BORG-PV likelihood. This likelihood was developped by Deaglan J. Bartelett during the beginning of my PhD
and it describes how well we can infer the supernova velocities. We start by defining the likelihood for one supernova indexed by $i$. 
We denote $\mu_{\text{obs},i}$ the observed distance modulus\footnote{As we explained in Chapter 2, we do not directly observe the distance modulus but instead we calculate it through
the observations of supernova lightcurves. Here we assume that $\mu_{\text{obs},i}$ is the distance modulus obtained after having observed the supernova lightcurves.}, 
$z_{\text{obs}}$ the observed redshift and $\sigma_{\mu}$ is the intrinsic dispersion of supernovae. 
In order to determine the likelihood, we need a prediction 
for the observed redshift. This should include the contribution of the supernova peculiar velocities with the help of the inferred velocity field. For objects at low redshift, 
Equation \ref{eqn:physical_distance} for comoving distances $r$ can be approximated as (see \cite{Weinberg_1972} page 485):
\begin{equation}
    r \approx \frac{c}{H_0} \left[ z - \frac{1+q_0}{2} z^2 \right]
\end{equation}
with $q_0 = \frac{\Omega_m}{2} - \Omega_{\Lambda} $. By inverting this relation for $z$, we get:
\begin{equation}
    z = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 2rH_0(1+q_0)/c}}{1+q_0}
    \label{eqn:zcosmo}
\end{equation} 
For our analysis, we fix $H_0$ and $\Omega_{\Lambda}$ to a fiducial cosmology. We can then infer the cosmological redshift by knowing the distance $r$, 
given by inverting the luminosity distance relation in Equation \ref{eqn:dL_mu}, and $\Omega_m$. We 
will be denoting this redshift as $z_{\text{cosmo}}$. We can have a predicted redshift by adding the contribution of the velocity field using the following relation:
\begin{equation}
    cz_{\text{pred},i}(r_i) = cz_{\text{cosmo},i}(r_i) + [1+z_{\text{cosmo},i}(r_i)]v_r
\end{equation}
where $z_{\text{pred}, i}$ is the predicted redshift and $v_r$ is the radial projection of the velocity field at a distance $r_i$. 
We also assume that the peculiar velocities can be 
affected by sources other than the velocity field which will be modeled as a Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_v)$, with $\sigma_v$ the velocity dispersion.\\ 

The second ingredient would be to define a prior on the supernova distribution. If we assume a constant density of 
supernovae, then the expected number of supernovae $N$ present at a distance $r$ is purely geometrical giving us:
\begin{equation}
    dN \propto 4\pi r^2 dr
\end{equation}
This means that the number of supernovae present between $r$ and $r+dr$ scales as $r^2$. Moreover, we define a supernova bias model relating the positions of supernovae with 
the evolved matter density field $\delta^F$ as:
\begin{equation}
    b_{\text{SN}}(r) = (1 + \delta^F(r))^{\alpha}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a free bias parameter. The bias parameter can exhibit four regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha=0$: No bias exists and supernova do not have a preferential position regarding the dark matter field.
    \item $\alpha=1$: Supernova exhibits a linear bias where the number of supernova is proportional to the overdensities.
    \item $\alpha>1$: Supernova have a higher preference of being in overdense regions.
    \item $\alpha<1$: Supernova have a higher preference of being in underdense regions.
\end{itemize}
The prior on the supernova positions can then be written as:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{SN}}(r) = r^2 b_{\text{SN}}(r) = r^2 (1 + \delta^F(r))^{\alpha}
\end{equation}
This prior plays a central role in correcting the inhomogeneous Malmquist bias. Rather than assuming a spatially homogeneous supernova population, 
the prior explicitly allows the expected supernova distribution to trace the reconstructed matter density field through the supernova bias 
model $ b_{\text{SN}}(r)$. 
In a flux-limited survey, incorporating this source distribution directly into the likelihood naturally accounts for the inhomogeneous Malmquist bias, 
avoiding the need for an external correction based on a homogeneous source distribution.

Thirdly, we define a selection function that accounts for the fact that supernovae are not observed infinitely far away. For this, we use the following selection function:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{selection}}(r) = e^{-\lambda \frac{r}{R_{\text{max}}}}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is a free parameter controlling the rate of decay and $R_{\text{max}}$ is the maximum comoving distance of the survey. 
This parametrisation allows for a smooth exponential decay of the expected number of supernovae with distance, avoiding the sharp 
discontinuity that would arise from a hard cutoff at $r = R_{\text{max}}$. \\

Finally, we marginalize over the distance $r$ for the supernova and normalize the likelihood, giving us:

\begin{equation}
    p_i(cz_{\text{obs},i}|\mu_{\text{obs},i})= \frac{\left( \int_{0}^{R_{\text{lim}}} dr \quad e^{- \frac{\left(\mu_{\text{pred},i}(r) -\frac{\mu_{\text{obs}},i}{\mu_A} \right)^2}{2\sigma_{\mu}^2} } 
    \quad e^{-\frac{(cz_{\text{pred},i}(r) - cz_{\text{obs},i})^2}{2 \sigma_v^2}} \quad p_{\text{SN}}(r) \quad e^{-\frac{\lambda}{R_{\text{max}}}r} \right)}{\left(\int_{0}^{R_{\text{lim}}} dr 
    \quad e^{- \frac{\left(\mu_{\text{pred},i}(r) -\frac{\mu_{\text{obs},i}}{\mu_A} \right)^2}{2\sigma_{\mu}^2} } 
    \quad p_{\text{SN}}(r) \quad e^{-\frac{\lambda}{R_{\text{max}}}r}\right)}
\end{equation}

Where $R_{\text{lim}}$ is the integration limit that is greater than the furthest supernova, $\mu_A$ the distance uncertainty and $\mu_{\text{pred}}(r)$ is calculated with:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\text{pred},i}(r_i) = 5 \log_{10} \left[ (1+z_{\text{cosmo},i}(r_i))r_i \right] + 25
    \label{eqn:mu_r}
\end{equation}

Assuming all supernovae are independent and behave the same, then the full BORG-PV likelihood then becomes

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \prod_{i=1}^{N_{\text{SN}}} p_i(cz_{\text{obs},i}|\mu_{\text{obs},i})
\end{equation}
Where $N_{\text{SN}}$ is the total number of supernovae considered.

\subsection{Generating self-consistent mocks}

The self-consistent supernova mocks are simulated to mach the model description above. We start by generating a random Gaussian initial density field $\delta^{IC}$ and let it evolve 
using a gravity solver until we obtain a final density field $\delta^{F}$ and a velocity field $\mathbf{v}$. We then define an amplitude function $\Phi$ containing the bias model and 
selection effects for a given $\alpha$, $\lambda$ and $R_{\text{max}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(r, \phi, \theta) = \left[1+ \delta(r, \phi, \theta)\right]^{\alpha}.e^{-\frac{\lambda}{R_{\text{max}}}r}
\end{equation}
The supernovae are then generated by a Poisson process where a discrete generation of the cartesian coordinates from the amplitude function $\Phi$ is done. 
The number of supernovae generated is chosen beforehand. A mask is applied to ensure that all the supernova generated are within the limit $R_{\text{lim}}$. We then 
calculate the observed redshift using:
\begin{equation}
    cz_{\text{obs}} = cz_{\text{cosmo}} + (1+z_{\text{cosmo}})v_r + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_v)
\end{equation}
where $z_{\text{cosmo}}$ is calculated using Equation \ref{eqn:zcosmo}, $v_r$ is the radial projection of the velocity field at $r$ for each supernova and 
$\sigma_v$ is the velocity dispersion that we fix. 
The distance modulus 
is obtained the same way as Equation \ref{eqn:mu_r} and is then scattered so we get an observed distance modulus with:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\text{obs}} = \mu_{\text{true}} + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\mu})
\end{equation}
Finally, the right ascension and declination angles are reported by converting the cartesian coordinates into spherical coordinates. \\

\subsubsection{Supernova prior}

Recent results from \cite{Lordet_2026} have shown that supernovae tend to be found in overly dense regions of the Universe with a large number of supernovae being present at a 
redshift of $z \approx 0.02-0.03$ creating what we see as a "bump" in the supernovae distribution accross redshift. This means that the assumption that the supernovae density is 
constant at low redshift (example of what was done in \cite{Perley_2020}) is not the most accurate description of the supernova distribution. The number of supernova 
$N_{\text{SN}}$ observed by a survey between a distance $r$ and $r+dr$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    dN = n(r) \times 4 \pi r^2 dr
\end{equation}
where $n(r)$ is the number density of supernovae. This can be related to the observed rate as:
\begin{equation}
    R(r) = \mathrm{S}(\phi, \theta, r) \frac{n(r)}{T_{\text{obs}}}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{obs}}$ is the time of observation and $\mathrm{S}$ is a selection function that would account for the survey footprint, galactic extinction and any 
instrumental effects that would decrease the number of observed supernovae. For our application on self-consistent simulations and the 
ZTF-Uchuu mocks, we generate supernovae on the full-sky and do not include any Malmquist bias effects. Therefore $\mathrm{S}$ becomes unity. 
If we assume a constant rate, then $dN \propto r^2 dr$ giving the $r^2$ geometric dependence in the prior described earlier. 
If we assume that this is not the case, then we get:
\begin{equation}
    dN(r) \propto n(r)r^2 dr
\end{equation}
In practice, we do not have direct access to $n(r)$ but we can infer $n(z)$. In fact, we can relate the two number densities with:
\begin{equation}
    n(r) \propto \frac{n(z)}{4\pi r^2 \times |\frac{dr}{dz}|}
    \label{eqn:n_r_new}
\end{equation}
The $|\frac{dr}{dz}|$ term is Jacobian for the transformation distance to redshift, and it can be deduced from Equation \ref{eqn:physical_distance} and is entirely 
determined by the cosmology. We get $\frac{dr}{dz} = \frac{c}{H(z)}$. Finally, we can present a more realistic prior on supernovae expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{SN}}(r) \propto r^2\, n(r)\,(1+\delta^F)^{\alpha},
    \quad \text{with } n(r) \, \text{expressed by Equation \ref{eqn:n_r_new}}.
    \label{eqn:new_prior}
\end{equation}
Finally, we fit the inferred $n(r)$ with a smooth differentiable function that is then implemented in the likelihood as seen in Figure \ref{fig:n_r_test}.

\subsection{Improved likelihood}

Our first improvement to the likelihood is the implementation of the 
prior $p_{\text{SN}}$ presented in Equation \ref{eqn:new_prior}. The 
second improvement concerns the selection function $p_{\text{selection}}(r)$, 
which we replace with a more rigorous formulation inspired by 
\cite{Stiskalek_2025}, following the framework of \cite{Kelly_2008}. 
In that work, a selection function is derived for 
a redshift-limited survey by modeling the probability that a supernova 
is detected as a smooth function of its predicted redshift. We adopt the same approach here, as detailed 
below.\footnote{\cite{Stiskalek_2025} applied this 
selection function framework to revisit the $H_0$ measurement from 
Cepheid-hosted supernovae, using the Manticore-Local density and 
velocity field reconstruction (\cite{Mcalpine_2025}). They obtained 
$H_0 = 71.1 \pm 1.4$ km.s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$, approximately $1.3\sigma$ 
lower than the SH0ES result (\cite{Riess_2022}) and in $2.8\sigma$ 
tension with the CMB, demonstrating the importance of rigorous selection 
function and velocity field modeling in distance ladder measurements.}

Given an observed dataset $\mathbf{d}_i$ for a supernova $i$ described by parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (such as $\alpha$, $\sigma_v$, etc...) 
and a selection function $S$ such that $S=1$ the data is observed and $S=0$ if not, then the general expression of the likelihood marked by a Poisson process is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} \propto \prod_{i=1}^{N_{\text{SN}}} \frac{p(\mathbf{d}_i, S=1 | \boldsymbol{\theta})}{p(S=1 | \boldsymbol{\theta})}
\end{equation}
where $p(\mathbf{d}_i, S=1 | \boldsymbol{\theta})$ denotes the likelihood for an individual supernova and $p(S=1 | \boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the probability for any supernova to 
be selected. We can show using Bayes' theorem that:
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{d}_i, S=1 | \boldsymbol{\theta}) = p(\mathbf{d}_i|r_i, \boldsymbol{\theta})p(S=1|r_i, \boldsymbol{\theta})p_{\text{SN}}(r_i)
\end{equation}
and since our dataset contains all supernovae below the redshift threshold $z=0.06$ (volume limited sample) then $p(S=1|r_i, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \approx 1$. Moreover, we marginalize over the distance as 
we did before and given that the observable is the supernova redshift we get:
\begin{equation}
    p(cz_{\text{obs},i}, S=1 | \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int_{0}^{R_{\text{lim}}} p(cz_{\text{obs},i}|r, \boldsymbol{\theta}, \mu_{\text{obs},i})p_{\text{SN}}(r) dr
\end{equation}
The paper also gives the exact formulation when 
the selection is done on redshift, which is exactly the case for the ZTF DR2 volume limited sample. In that case, the fraction of detected samples 
is given by Equation (33) in the paper:
\begin{equation}
    p(S=1| \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int p_{\text{SN}}(\mathbf{r}) \Phi_{\text{CDF}} \left(\frac{cz_{\text{thresh}} - cz_{\text{pred}(r)}}{\sigma_v}\right) d^3\mathbf{r}
\end{equation}
where $z_{\text{thresh}}$ is the limiting redshift ($z_{\text{thresh}}=0.06$ in our case) and $\Phi_{\text{CDF}}$ the normal cumulative distribution function. The full likelihood 
is therefore given by:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \prod_{i=1}^{N_\text{SN}} \frac{p(cz_{\text{obs},i}|\mu_{\text{obs},i})}{p(S=1)}
\end{equation}
Or more simply, we can take the negative logarithm of the likelihood which gives us:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{ - \log \mathcal{L} =  - \sum_{i=1}^{N_\text{SN}} p(cz_{\text{obs},i}|\mu_{\text{obs},i}) + N_\text{SN}p(S=1)}
    \label{eqn:borg_pv_L}
\end{equation}
Where $p(cz_{\text{obs}_i}|\mu_{\text{obs}_i})$ is the likelihood of each supernova with no selection effects which is given by
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{p(cz_{\text{obs}_i}|\mu_{\text{obs}_i}) = \int_{0}^{R_{\text{lim}}} dr \quad e^{- \frac{\left(\mu_{\text{pred},i}(r) -\frac{\mu_{\text{obs},i}}{\mu_A} \right)^2}{2\sigma_{\mu}^2} } 
    \quad e^{-\frac{(cz_{\text{pred},i}(r) - cz_{\text{obs},i})^2}{2 \sigma_v^2}} \quad r^2\, n(r)\,(1+\delta^F(r))^{\alpha}}
\end{equation}
