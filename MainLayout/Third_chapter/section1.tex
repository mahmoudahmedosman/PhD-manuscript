\section{The \textsc{Lemaître} project}

In Chapter 1 we have discussed the so-called $w_0 - w_a$ tension, namely the apparent preference
for a time-varying dark-energy equation of state emerging from
recent joint analyses of supernovae, BAO, and CMB data (\cite{Brout_2022}; \cite{Rubin_2023}; \cite{DES_2024} and \cite{DESI_DR2_2025}).
The deviation from $\Lambda$CDM appears to originate at low redshift, where 
only supernova data contribute, and the samples used contain only a few 
hundred objects. These supernova samples are heterogeneous legacy compilations 
with significant overlap between the different analyses mentioned above.

The \textsc{Lemaître} project consists of constructing one of the largest pre-LSST Hubble diagrams by using an independent set of supernovae. 
The goal of the \textsc{Lemaître} project is to address this tension by providing independent constraints of the Dark Energy equation of
state based on: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a distinct supernova dataset, unused in previous cosmological 
    analyses, with a rich low-$z$ sample
    \item a metrology and flux-calibration programme designed to control 
    measurement systematics at the per-mil level
    \item a new cosmological inference pipeline delivering an independent 
    measurement of the dark energy equation of state
\end{enumerate}
The project faces two main challenges. The first is managing flux metrology at the per-mil level. Figure \ref{fig:w_variation} represents the 
deviation of the distance modulus due to a variation of w of 2\% and 10\% as a function of redshift. In order to obtain per-cent level 
constraints on $w$, distance modulus measurements must be accurate at 
the per-mil level. The second challenge is simplifying the inference chain 
through the introduction of the Georges pipeline, described in 
Section \ref{sec:georges}, to which I contributed during this PhD 
(see Chapter 4).\\

\section{Datasets: ZTF, SNLS, HSC}

The \textsc{Lema\^{i}tre} project uses three supernova datasets: ZTF, 
SNLS, and HSC, covering a wide redshift range from $z_{\text{min}} \sim 0.02$ 
to $z_{\text{max}} \sim 1.5$. Limiting the analysis to three datasets 
significantly improves the homogeneity of the cosmological sample compared 
to previous analyses, reducing the number of instruments from 25 to 3 and 
the number of bandpasses from 100 to 12. Each instrument has undergone a 
dedicated metrological programme in which the filters have been characterised 
to a precision of a few $\AA$.

The cosmological sample comprises roughly 3000 Type Ia supernovae, the 
majority of which come from the ZTF survey at low redshift.

Treating the three surveys homogeneously is essential. Since they cover 
different redshift ranges, any survey-dependent bias in the analysis or 
calibration will manifest as a redshift-dependent systematic, potentially 
biasing the cosmological results. The photometry and calibration of each 
survey are therefore handled in the same way within the pipeline. The three 
surveys have overlapping sky footprints, which greatly facilitates 
cross-calibration. Each dataset is described in greater detail in the 
following sections.

\subsection{ZTF survey} \label{sec:ztf}
We start off with the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF) survey as well as the supernova sample that was primarily studied in this PhD the ZTF DR2. 
The ZTF camera is mounted at the prime focus of the 48-inch Samuel 
Oschin Telescope at Palomar Observatory as seen in Figure \ref{fig:palomar}. The telescope follows a 
Schmidt optical design, specifically developed to provide a very wide 
field of view. Its optical system combines a 1.83m spherical primary 
mirror with an aspheric corrector lens located at the entrance pupil, 
whose effective diameter is approximately 1.24m. This configuration 
delivers a field of view of approximately 47 $\text{deg}^2$. The field of view can be seen in 
Figure \ref{fig:ztf_fov}. The focal surface 
lies roughly halfway between the corrector and the primary mirror and 
is intrinsically curved. While this geometry was well suited to the 
curved photographic plates for which the telescope was originally 
designed, substantial modifications were required to accommodate a 
modern mosaic of planar CCDs. \\

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{MainLayout/Third_chapter/figures/palomar-observatory.jpg}
    \caption{Mount Palomar Observatory. Credits: Caltech \url{https://www.ztf.caltech.edu/} }
    \label{fig:palomar}
\end{figure}

The ZTF science camera consists of 16 e2v CCD231-C6 detectors, each 
comprising 6144×6160 pixels. The CCDs are mounted on a faceted cold 
plate that approximates the curved focal surface. The residual field 
curvature is corrected through a combination of the spherical cryostat 
window and an individual plano-convex field-flattening lens placed 
immediately in front of each detector. This design provides nearly 
uniform image quality across the full focal plane. 

The camera is equipped with three broadband filters: $g$, $r$, and $i$, 
approximately covering the wavelength ranges 400–550nm, 550–700nm, 
and 700–900nm, respectively. 
The transmissions of the filters are sensor-dependent and we display them by averaging over the sensor transmissions in Figure \ref{fig:ztf_filters}. 
The filters are mounted immediately in 
front of the cryostat window and are selected by a robotic 
filter-exchange system installed inside the telescope tube. 

The effective system throughput is detector-dependent, primarily 
because two different anti-reflection coatings are used across the 
focal plane. The eight CCDs in the upper and lower rows have a 
single-layer coating optimized for the $g$ and $r$ bands, whereas the 
eight CCDs in the two central rows have a two-layer coating providing 
enhanced quantum efficiency at short wavelengths. This difference 
introduces a measurable spatial dependence of the instrumental 
passbands across the focal plane. Figure \ref{fig:ztf_cadence} shows the 
focal-plane-averaged throughput in the three ZTF bands. 

For 30-s exposures obtained under dark-sky conditions, the typical 5$\sigma$ 
point-source limiting magnitudes are $m_g \sim 20.8-21.1$mag, $m_r \sim 20.6-20.9$mag, $m_i \sim 19.9-20.2$mag with shallower limits under 
bright lunar conditions. Further details of the ZTF observing system 
are presented in \cite{Dekany_2020}. 

The ZTF telescope is fully robotic, and the entire science focal plane 
can be read out in approximately 8.2s. Combined with 30-s exposures, 
rapid telescope slews, and the 47 $\text{deg}^2$ field of view, this enables 
observing rates of at least 85 fields per hour, corresponding to 3760 $\text{deg}^2$ per 
hour or twice the extragalactic sky per night. These characteristics 
make ZTF a particularly powerful facility for the discovery and 
high-cadence monitoring of optical transients. \\

\subsubsection{Spectroscopic follow-up}

ZTF is accompanied by a spectrograph named the Spectral Energy Distribution Machine (SEDM) located in the same observatory on the P60 telescope. The purpose of the object to 
conduct a follow-up on the photometry in order to properly classify the transients observed. Its limiting magnitude is 20.5mag. This follow-up played an important role in acquiring the 
60\% spectra for the ZTF Bright Transient Survey (BTS) for which the magnitude limit was 18.5mag, a limited fraction of the ZTF total survey. \\


\subsubsection{ZTF DR2}

ZTF has been in operation since 2018 and has discovered approximately
10,000 Type Ia supernovae over the redshift range ($0.002<z<0.1$), with
densely sampled light curves in the $g$, $r$, and $i$ bands. The
resulting sample is highly homogeneous and approximately
volume-limited up to $z\simeq 0.06$. 

Within the \textsc{Lemaître} project, we focus on the subsample collected
during the first three years of the survey. This dataset has already
been released as the ZTF Second Supernova Data Release (DR2; \cite{Rigault_2020}).


We present in this subsection the Type Ia supernova data release of ZTF named DR2. Figure \ref{fig:ztf_sn_picture} shows images of a supernova observed by ZTF. 
The sample contains 3628 
spectroscopically identified supernovae up to a redshift of $z \approx 0.1$. Figure \ref{fig:ztf_lcs} shows examples of the sampling of the supernova observed by ZTF in DR2. We must note that not all supernova data is 
"good" enough to be used for a cosmological analysis. For such an analysis, quality cuts on sampling around the maximum emission of light, the color and the stretch must be applied. 
The details on the cosmology quality cuts can be found in \cite{Rigault_2020}. After the cuts, 
the sample maintains 2667 supernovae that pass. An example of what the ZTF DR2 Hubble diagram looks like can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:ztf_dr2_hubble}. A subset of the sample named the "volume-limited" sample contains all supernovae up to a redshift of $z=0.06$. 
This represents a complete sample of supernovae, i.e. the sample is not influenced by any selection effects due to the magnitude limits of the telescope. This subset contains 994 supernovae. 
There are roughly twenty papers released from the ZTF collaboration published with the release of DR2 amongst them are \cite{Ginolin_2025_a, Ginolin_2025_b}.\\

However, the current DR2 photometric calibration is accurate only at 
approximately the 5\% level, is insufficient for precision cosmology. 
The Lemaître project therefore includes a complete reprocessing of the 
dataset, combining scene-modeling photometry with a dedicated 
flux-calibration chain, with the aim of achieving sub-percent—and 
ultimately per-mille-level—accuracy in flux. 

As part of the \textsc{Lemaître} project, the ZTF instrumental throughput was 
measured in situ using a Traveling Collimated Beam Projector (TCBP, Van den 
Abbele et al, in prep). 
The TCBP consists of a calibrated monochromatic light source that 
injects a well-characterized collimated beam into the telescope 
optical system. This measurement campaign enabled the spatial 
variations of the ZTF throughput across the focal plane to be mapped, 
and detector-specific throughput curves to be derived for each CCD, 
with a wavelength-calibration accuracy of approximately 
1$\text{\AA}$. \\


\subsection{SNLS survey} \label{sec:snls}


The second dataset is provided by the Supernova Legacy Survey (SNLS; 
\cite{Astier_2006}), a five-year Type Ia supernova survey conducted 
with the MegaPrime/MegaCam imaging system (\cite{Aune_2003}). An image of the MegaCam system is seen in Figure \ref{fig:megacam}. 
MegaCam is mounted at the prime focus of the 3.6-m 
Canada–France–Hawaii Telescope (CFHT), seen in Figure \ref{fig:cfht}, and provides a field of view of 
approximately 1 $\text{deg}^2$, 
a much smaller footprint than ZTF that limits the survey from conducting wide scans. Instead, deep observations are done 
in four fields equally distributed up to a redshift of 1. The fields D1, D2, D3 and D4 coordinates are summarized in Table \ref{tab:snls_field}.
A four-element wide-field corrector delivers 
nearly uniform image quality across the focal plane.

The focal plane of the MegaCam camera is made up of 40 CCDs (model e2v 
CCD42-90) each having 2048 × 4612 pixels (Figure \ref{fig:megacam}). It is read out 
in about 40 seconds, with a readout noise of about 5 $\text{e}^-$. The camera is 
equiped with 5 broadband filters, $u, g, r, i, z$ (Figure \ref{fig:snls_filters}). The time-domain 
SNLS observations themselves were obtained in the $g$, $r$, $i$ and $z$ 
bands; the $u$ -band observations belonged to the CFHTLS Deep 
programme and were not time-sequenced for SNLS. Two versions of the 
$i$-filter were used during the survey, after the original filter was 
accidentally damaged in 2007\footnote{The $i$ filter was broken during SNLS observation period and was replaced by a similar 
filter, the $i2$}. 

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.1\textwidth]{MainLayout/Third_chapter/figures/snls_filters.pdf}
    \caption{SNLS filters. Dotted lines represent transmissions for different sensors. Solid lines represent the average transmissions over all sensors.}
    \label{fig:snls_filters}
\end{figure}


All MegaCam filters exhibit significant spatial variations in their 
transmission. Unlike the ZTF case, where CCD-to-CCD variations in 
quantum efficiency play an important role, the MegaCam 
non-uniformities arise predominantly from spatial variations in the 
filter transmission, in particular shifts in the effective central 
wavelength associated with coating and manufacturing non-uniformities. 
These variations were characterized through laboratory measurements 
and mapped on sky using dithered observations of dense stellar fields 
(\cite{Regnault_2009}; \cite{Betoule_2013}; \cite{Sassolas_2018}). 

The combination of large mirror, a wide field and a relatively fast 
readout enabled MegaCam to scan large enough volumes of Universe to 
discover and follow-up hundreds of supernovae at intermediate and high 
redshifts. The survey repeatedly imaged four one-square-degree fields, 
typically every three to four nights during dark time, producing 
well-sampled multiband light curves for approximately 500 
spectroscopically confirmed Type Ia supernovae over the redshift range 
$0.2<z<1.0$. 

For the \textsc{Lemaître} analysis, a dedicated calibration program was carried out during engineering time to anchor the 
supernova observations directly to primary flux calibrators. The precision of this anchoring is 
of 2 to 5 mmag, depending on the band. In addition, the MegaCam filters 
were remeasured on a dedicated bench (\cite{Sassolas_2018}). This measurement campaign allowed to remap the sizeable non-uniformities of each 
filter, and to determine focal-plane position-dependent filters with an accuracy of 2 to 4 $\AA$, 
depending on the band. 
The 4th and 5th years have not yet been published and will be in (Betoule et al. in prep.). The number is expected to be around 300 supernovae from SNLS 
that will be used in \textsc{Lemaître}. 


\subsection{HSC survey} \label{sec:hsc}


The final dataset comes from the Hyper Suprime-Cam Subaru Strategic Program (\cite{Aihara_2022}), also denoted HSCSSP. 
It uses the Hyper Suprime Cam (HSC), a camera mounted on the Subaru 8.2m telescope (Figure \ref{fig:hsc}) with a field of view of 1.8 $\text{deg}^2$. 
The Subaru telescope is located on top of the Mauna 
Kea volcano in Hawaii, just like CFHT. 


\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{MainLayout/Third_chapter/figures/hscsubaru.jpg}
    \caption{The HSC camera on top of the Subaru telescope. Image from \url{https://hsc.mtk.nao.ac.jp/ssp/gallery/}}
    \label{fig:hsc}
\end{figure}


The main focus of HSCSSP is to build a galaxy 
catalog for weak lensing analysis, however the observation cadence of its deep fields were adapted for supernovae observation. 
The HSC observations are conducted 
in 7 photometric filters: $g$, $r$, $r2$, $i$, $i2$, $z$ and $Y$. The bands are more uniform than MegaCam's bands as seen in Figure \ref{fig:hsc_filters} 
with the reconstruction of the $r$ and $i$ bands. 

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.1\textwidth]{MainLayout/Third_chapter/figures/hsc_filters.pdf}
    \caption{HSC filters. Dotted lines represent transmissions for different sensors. Solid lines represent the average transmissions over all sensors.}
    \label{fig:hsc_filters}
\end{figure}


The sample contains roughly 300 Type Ia supernovae covering the redshift range $0.8<1.2$, the highest range in \textsc{Lemaître}. 
This is made possible by the deep-depletion, red-sensitive HSC CCDs, which provide high signal-to-noise observations in the $z$ and $Y$ bands. 
The HSC bandpasses were measured on a dedicated bench using a monochromatic beam designed to reproduce the geometry of the science beam (\cite{Satoshi_2018}). 
The redshifts of the HSC supernovae are determined by measuring the redshift of their
host galaxies. They initially measured with the AAT/AAOmega and DESI instruments and completed by observations from the Subaru/FOCAS and VLT instruments
(Suzuki et al. in prep). 
Supernova observations are done in the deep and ultra-deep fields of HSC named SXDS and COSMOS. They cover the MegaCam fields and are in the ZTF footprint


With those three datasets, we aim at building a Hubble diagram within \textsc{Lemaître} as seen in Figure \ref{fig:hubble_lemaitre}. 
The analysis pipeline from the extraction of the light curves up to the cosmological 
inference will be present in the following Section \ref{sec:pipeline}.
